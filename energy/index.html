Energy & Power: The Kilowatt-Hour Problem

Energy and power are the most commonly confused physical quantities in everyday conversation — and in engineering procurement. Energy (measured in joules, kilowatt-hours, calories, BTU) is a quantity — the total amount of work done or heat transferred. Power (measured in watts, horsepower) is a rate — the speed at which energy is transferred. Confusing kW (power) with kWh (energy) is the single most expensive unit mistake in engineering procurement. A generator rated at 100 kW can deliver 100 kilowatt-hours in one hour — not "100 kilowatts per hour."

James Watt defined the horsepower in the 1780s as a marketing tool: he needed to sell steam engines to mine owners who were used to buying horses. His calculation — 1 hp = 33,000 foot-pounds per minute — was deliberately generous, overestimating what a horse could actually sustain. The modern mechanical horsepower is 745.69987158227022 watts exactly. The metric horsepower (PS or CV) is 735.49875 W — close but different, and the source of countless incorrect car specifications.

The BTU: The Unit That Refuses to Retire

The British Thermal Unit (BTU) is defined as the energy required to raise the temperature of one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. Despite the SI system adopting the joule in 1960, the BTU remains the dominant unit in North American HVAC, natural gas trading, and building energy codes. One BTU is exactly 1,055.05585262 joules (IT definition) — a number that, like the horsepower, betrays its ad-hoc origins. A typical residential air conditioner is rated at 12,000 BTU/h (1 "ton" of cooling, from the days when cooling was measured in tons of ice melted per day). In SI, that's 3.517 kW of thermal power. The natural gas market trades in MMBTU (one million BTU), and the Henry Hub natural gas spot price is quoted in dollars per MMBTU. Converting between therms (100,000 BTU), MMBTU, and gigajoules (GJ, approximately 0.9478 MMBTU) is a daily necessity for energy traders, and a units error in this chain has caused seven-figure trading losses when automated systems misinterpreted the unit of a counterparty's quote.

Household Energy: What a Kilowatt-Hour Actually Means

The average US household consumes approximately 10,500 kWh of electricity per year — about 29 kWh per day, or 104.4 megajoules. That energy, if converted entirely to mechanical work, could lift a 100-tonne locomotive 105 meters vertically — every day. A refrigerator draws about 150 W continuously (1.3 MWh/year). An LED lightbulb uses 10 W — 1 kWh after 100 hours. An electric oven at 3 kW running for one hour consumes 3 kWh, costing roughly $0.45 at average US residential rates. The unit confusion between kW (the instantaneous power draw) and kWh (the cumulative energy used) is responsible for countless misread electric bills. A device drawing 1 kW for 1 hour costs the same as a device drawing 100 W for 10 hours — they both consume 1 kWh. Most household energy disputes stem from not understanding this relationship.

Renewable Energy at Grid Scale: Terawatt-Hours to Joules

Global electricity consumption in 2025 was approximately 30,000 TWh (terawatt-hours). In joules, that's 1.08 × 10²⁰ J — a number so large it's nearly meaningless without comparison. One terawatt-hour (1 TWh = 3.6 × 10¹⁵ J) is roughly the annual electricity consumption of 90,000 US households. A single large nuclear reactor (1 GW) generates about 8.7 TWh per year at 100% capacity factor. The Three Gorges Dam, the world's largest power station, generated approximately 111.8 TWh in 2020 — about 0.4% of global electricity. Converting between these scales — individual household kWh, power plant TWh, and global EJ (exajoules, 10¹⁸ J) — requires fluency across six orders of magnitude in the energy unit ladder. Energy system modelers work in EJ/year for global scenarios (the IEA's World Energy Outlook uses EJ and Mtoe — million tonnes of oil equivalent, where 1 Mtoe = 41.868 PJ). The conversion chain from a 10 W LED bulb to the world's 600 EJ primary energy supply spans 16 orders of magnitude — a range that makes unit ambiguity a genuine risk for misinterpreted policy recommendations.
