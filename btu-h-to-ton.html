BTU/h to tons — the ice-trade unit that never left

A contractor says your house needs a "3-ton" system, and the number is a fossil. It counts cooling not in modern units but in tons of ice — the way buildings were chilled before electric compressors existed. The definition is clean and exact: one ton of refrigeration is 12,000 BTU/h. Where the Ton Came From

In the decades before mechanical refrigeration, an entire industry harvested lake and river ice in winter, stored it in sawdust-insulated icehouses, and sold it through the summer. Cooling capacity was quoted the way it was physically delivered: in tons of ice. One short ton — 2,000 pounds — of ice absorbs 144 BTU per pound as it melts, or 288,000 BTU in total. Spread that melting over a day and you get 288,000 ÷ 24 = 12,000 BTU per hour, the number that still rates every residential air conditioner in America. When Willis Carrier and the early compressor builders arrived, they kept the unit so customers could compare a machine against the ice they already understood.

Notice the ton is not a weight, and it does not tell you anything about the machine's physical size. A 3-ton system removes heat at the rate three tons of ice would, but it is a steel box weighing a few hundred pounds, not six thousand. The square-footage estimates in the table are rules of thumb that ignore climate, insulation, and ceiling height — a real load calculation runs through Manual J, and the ton rating is just its final answer, rounded to the nearest half-ton.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many tons is 12,000 BTU?

Exactly 1 ton. The ton of refrigeration is defined as 12,000 BTU/h, so 12,000 BTU/h is 1 ton, 18,000 BTU/h is 1.5 tons, and 24,000 BTU/h is 2 tons. The number is a definition, not a measurement, so it converts cleanly every time.

Why is a ton of cooling 12,000 BTU per hour?

It is a relic of the ice trade. Before mechanical refrigeration, buildings were cooled with harvested ice, and a 'ton' was a short ton — 2,000 pounds — of ice. Melting that much ice absorbs 2,000 × 144 = 288,000 BTU of heat. Spread over 24 hours, that is 288,000 ÷ 24 = 12,000 BTU per hour. The modern unit kept the name after the ice was replaced by compressors.

Is an AC ton a weight?

No. A refrigeration ton is a rate of heat removal — 12,000 BTU/h — not a mass. A '3-ton' central air conditioner does not weigh three tons; it removes heat at the rate that melting three tons of ice per day would. The word survives as a fossil from the ice-trade era, like horsepower surviving from the steam age.
