Engineering Context

Density links the mass and volume families on this site — it is their ratio. For the mass numerator, the kg to g and lbs to kg converters carry the same 1959 constants that appear here; for the volume denominator, the liters to mL and cubic feet to cubic meters pages supply the cubed lengths. The density hub collects all ten converters in this family.

This direction is what a metric design package needs when the concrete is placed by a US plant, and the round numbers on both sides are a trap. A metric mix design reads 2,400 kg/m³; the US equivalent reads 150 pcf; the exact conversion gives 149.8, not 150. Both conventions are nominal values chosen for convenience, and both are routinely exceeded or missed in the field, so the 0.2 difference is not an error to chase. What does matter is consistency: if a dead-load calculation uses 150 and the supplier invoices against 2,400, the two documents describe concretes that differ by 0.13 percent.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is water 62.4 lb/ft³?

Because a cubic foot of water weighs 62.4 pounds — a consequence of the 1959 pound and foot definitions, cubed. The exact value is 62.427961 lb/ft³ at maximum density. US engineers memorize 62.4 the way metric engineers memorize 1,000; both are the same water.

Is concrete really 150 lb/ft³?

Normal-weight concrete is commonly specified at 150 lb/ft³ (about 2,400 kg/m³). Lightweight concrete drops to 90-115 lb/ft³, and the mix density is a structural input — changing it changes the dead load and the footing size.

What about the 1959 foot?

The foot was defined as exactly 0.3048 meters by the 1959 agreement. Density cubes it: one cubic foot is exactly 0.028316846592 cubic meters. That's where the 16.018463 comes from.
