liters to cubic meters

The liter was defined in 1795 as exactly one cubic decimeter, and it has kept that definition through every SI revision since — including the 2019 redefinition of the kilogram, which touched everything else and left the liter untouched. One liter is one thousandth of a cubic meter, period. The practical consequence shows up everywhere metric volume is traded across scales: municipal water bills measure consumption in cubic meters, household containers measure in liters, and the conversion is a factor of 1,000 either way, never a rounded one.

cubic meters = liters × 0.001

Frequently Asked Questions

How many liters are in a cubic meter?

Exactly 1,000. The liter is defined as one cubic decimeter, and a cubic meter is (10 dm)³ = 1,000 dm³ = 1,000 L. This is definitional — the same reason a cubic meter of water weighs exactly 1,000 kg at its densest temperature.

Why do water bills use cubic meters instead of liters?

Because municipal-scale volumes become awkward in liters — a household's monthly use is often 10,000–30,000 L, which reads cleanly as 10–30 m³. Utility metering also avoids mixing the liter's volume definition with the kilogram's mass definition.

Is 1 liter of water really 1 kg?

At 4 °C (water's maximum density) and standard pressure, 1 L of pure water has a mass of 0.999972 kg — close enough to 1 kg that the original 1795 definition made them equal by design. The 2019 SI redefinition slightly decoupled the two, but the practical difference remains invisible outside metrology labs.
