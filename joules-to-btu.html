The Paperwork Problem

Every building-science calculation in the last half century has been done in SI: energy models, insulation R-value math, heat-load software, and efficiency regulations all output joules or their multiples. But the American HVAC trade — the people who buy and install the equipment — still sizes everything in BTU. So the working engineer lives in a constant state of translation, and the practical rule of thumb is one most of them carry in their heads: a megajoule is just under a thousand BTU. 1 MJ = 947.8 BTU, so "the house needs 63 MJ" instantly reads as "about a 60,000 BTU furnace." Frequently Asked Questions

How many BTU are in one joule?

One joule equals 0.000947817 BTU (IT) — roughly a thousandth. In practical terms, 1 kJ ≈ 0.948 BTU and 1 MJ ≈ 948 BTU. The factor comes from the defined relationship 1 BTU = 1,055.05585262 J, so it is exact, not a measurement.

Why would an engineer convert joules to BTU?

Because the report is written in SI but the equipment catalog and the installers work in BTU. A building heat-load study delivers megajoules; the furnace sales page lists BTU/h. Converting the scientific figure to the trade's unit is what lets a spec sheet become a purchase order — the same reason efficiency standards publish in both.

What is the easiest mental shortcut for joules to BTU?

Multiply kilojoules by 0.95 for a close BTU figure — 1 kJ ≈ 0.948 BTU, so the shortcut is good to half a percent. For megajoules, remember 1 MJ ≈ 948 BTU ≈ just under 1,000 BTU. When the exact number matters — equipment sizing, efficiency math — use the full factor 0.000947817 per joule.
