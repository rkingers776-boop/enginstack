watt-hours to kWh

Every battery on Earth is rated in watt-hours somewhere in its documentation — a phone at 15 Wh, a laptop at 60 Wh, an electric car at 60,000 Wh — and the kilowatt-hour, the unit the world buys electricity in, is simply 1,000 of them. The conversion is pure decimal arithmetic: divide watt-hours by 1,000 and you have kWh, with no constant, no approximation, and no room for error beyond misplaced zeros.

watt-hours to kWh

The watt-hour and the kilowatt-hour are the same unit at different decimal magnitudes — 1 kWh = 1,000 Wh by definition, exactly as 1 kW = 1,000 W. What makes this page worth a converter rather than a mental rule is the scale of modern batteries: an electric vehicle's 60,000 Wh pack is 60 kWh, a power station's 512 Wh is 0.512 kWh, and a home battery at 13,500 Wh is 13.5 kWh. Misplacing one zero when converting between them is a factor-of-ten error in a device's stated capacity.

kWh = watt-hours ÷ 1,000

Frequently Asked Questions

How many watt-hours are in a kWh?

Exactly 1,000. The kilo- prefix means 1,000 by definition, so 1 kWh = 1,000 Wh — the same relationship as kilowatt to watt or kilogram to gram.

Why are EV batteries rated in kWh but phone batteries in mAh?

EV batteries quote energy capacity in kWh (power × time). Phone batteries historically quote charge in mAh (current × time) because the voltage is fixed, so mAh is proportional to energy. To compare across devices, convert mAh × V ÷ 1,000 to get Wh.

How much does 1 kWh cost on my bill?

Typically $0.10–0.30 depending on region and time of day. That makes a 60 kWh EV battery worth roughly $6–18 of electricity — useful for estimating charging cost from the kWh number.
