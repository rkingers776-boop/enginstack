liters to cubic feet

A European refrigerator is sold in liters — 400 L, say — and an American one in cubic feet — 14.1 ft³. Same appliance, same interior space, two countries, one awkward conversion. The awkwardness is not a rounded approximation problem: the factor is exact, built from the 1959 foot (0.3048 m) cubed against the liter's cubic-decimeter definition. It is awkward only because nobody has a mental image of 0.0353 of anything.

liters to cubic feet

The liters-to-cubic-feet factor comes straight from the two definitions: 1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly (1959 agreement), so 1 ft³ = 0.028316846592 m³, and since 1 L = 0.001 m³, one cubic foot holds exactly 28.316846592 liters. Flip it and one liter is 0.0353146667 ft³. The number has ten significant digits because it inherits them from the treaty-defined foot. Fish tanks, refrigerator specs, and shipping-company volumetric charges all cross this bridge daily — and it never rounds to a friendly number, which is why the calculator exists.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many liters are in a cubic foot?

Exactly 28.316846592. It follows from 1 ft = 0.3048 m (exact, 1959 agreement): (0.3048 m)³ = 0.028316846592 m³, and 1 m³ = 1,000 L. So a cubic foot is 28.316846592 L — no rounding involved.

Why do fridges use liters in Europe and cubic feet in America?

Because each market sells to the habits of its buyers. Europeans think in liters for containers; Americans think in cubic feet for appliance interior volume, a habit inherited from the cubic-foot era of refrigerator design. The appliance is the same; the label unit differs.

How do I convert liters to cubic feet by hand?

Multiply liters by 0.0353 for a close mental estimate (within 0.02%). For exact work, use the full factor 0.0353146667 — or divide liters by 28.316846592, which gives the same result since the two factors are reciprocals.
