Acres to Hectares: A Medieval Ox, a French Revolutionary Committee, and a 2.47× Gap That Can Eat a Farm

In the 13th century, an English farmer looked at the furrow his oxen had plowed between breakfast and lunch and called it an acre. In 1795, a French revolutionary committee sat in a Paris chamber and defined the hectare as a clean 100 meters by 100 meters —10,000 square meters of Enlightenment rationality. The two units have never agreed. They differ by a factor of 2.47. That gap —large enough to misprice a $10 million farm, subtle enough to hide inside a contract's fine print —has been the mechanism behind cross-border land fraud, carbon credit misreporting, and at least one international arbitration that ended with a buyer discovering their "500-hectare" ranch was actually 500 acres, and worth 2.47 times less than what they paid.

The Furlong, the Chain, and the Tired Ox

Before England had surveyors, it had plowmen. The acre was not designed —it was observed. In medieval open-field agriculture, a single furrow ran the length of a field. The oxen pulled a heavy wooden plow through damp clay until they needed rest. That distance, standardized over centuries of use, became the furlong: 660 feet, or one-eighth of a mile. The width of the plow's cut —the turned soil behind the blade —was standardized at 4 rods. Four rods equal one chain: 66 feet. An acre was simply one furlong long by one chain wide: 660 × 66 = 43,560 square feet.

That number —43,560 —was codified in English statute under Edward I in the late 13th century. It has not changed in 700 years. The Statutum de Admensuratione Terre (Statute for the Measuring of Land), issued circa 1300, formalized the acre, the rod, the furlong, and the chain into a coherent system. The surveyor's chain —66 feet of 100 links, each link 7.92 inches —became the tool of English land measurement for the next six centuries. A cricket pitch is exactly one chain long to this day —22 yards between the wickets —because the same surveyor's chain that laid out English farms also laid out English cricket grounds.

The acre survived the Industrial Revolution, the colonization of North America, the American Revolution, and the metric reforms of the 19th and 20th centuries. The Homestead Act of 1862 granted 160 acres —one quarter-section of the US Public Land Survey System grid —to any citizen who would farm it for five years. The grid is still visible from airplane windows across the American Midwest: square miles divided into four 160-acre quarter-sections, the ghost of a 19th-century land distribution program etched into the geometry of the continent.

The Revolution That Measured the Earth in Tens

While England was exporting its ox-defined acre to North America, France was having a revolution —and revolutions are not kind to inherited units of measurement. The metric system, decreed by the French National Convention in 1795, was a project of the Enlightenment: rational, universal, decimal. The meter was defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator along the meridian through Paris —a definition that required two astronomers, Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain, to spend seven years surveying from Dunkirk to Barcelona during the chaos of the Revolutionary Wars.

The are —100 square meters —was the metric unit for land area. The hectare, meaning "100 ares," is a square 100 meters on each side: exactly 10,000 m². No oxen. No furlongs. No fractions. A hectare is the area of a square with sides you can walk in a minute and a half. It is exactly 0.01 square kilometers —100 hectares to the square kilometer, clean decimal scaling from a garden to a continent.

The hectare was adopted by almost every country that adopted the metric system. France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Brazil, India, China, Australia —all measure farmland in hectares. The United States, the United Kingdom, Canada (partially), and a handful of former British colonies retain the acre. The acre-to-hectare conversion —ha = acres × 0.40468564224 —is the narrow bridge across a 200-year-old philosophical divide between two ways of measuring the world: one rooted in a tired ox's afternoon, the other in the distance from the North Pole to the equator.

Worked Examples

1 acre —0.4047 ha

1 × 0.40468564224 = 0.4047 ha. Two suburban house lots. A football field without the end zones (1.32 acres makes a full FIFA pitch). The American mental benchmark for "a piece of land you can stand in the middle of and see all four edges."

160 acres —64.75 ha

160 × 0.40468564224 = 64.75 ha. The Homestead Act parcel. A quarter-section of the PLSS grid. Enough for a family farm in 1862. Still the standard unit of agricultural land subdivision in the US and Canada. A Canadian quarter-section (160 acres) is 64.7 ha —and a Saskatchewan farmer who talks about "my section" means 640 acres, or 259 hectares.

640 acres —259.0 ha

640 × 0.40468564224 = 259.0 ha. One square mile. One PLSS section. The fundamental unit of Jefferson's grid —the rectilinear survey that Thomas Jefferson designed to divide the American frontier into orderly, sellable squares. Visible from any flight over the Great Plains or the Central Valley of California.

1,000 acres —404.7 ha

1,000 × 0.40468564224 = 404.7 ha. A small cattle ranch. 10—5 minutes to drive across in a pickup truck. At $5,000/acre (2024 Iowa farmland median), this is a $5 million transaction. The difference between calling it 1,000 acres and 405 hectares is cosmetic. The difference between calling it 405 hectares and 405 acres is $3.7 million.

The 2.47 Factor: Where the Math Gets Weaponized

A hectare is 2.47105 times larger than an acre. The ratio is not 2:1. It's not 2.5:1. It's an irrational-looking decimal —2.47105381467... —because the acre's definition divides a square mile by a clean number (640) and the hectare divides a square kilometer by a clean number (100), and the mile and the kilometer are not rationally related. The mile is 1,609.344 meters because the foot is 0.3048 meters exactly and a mile is 5,280 feet. Every digit in 2.47105381467... is a direct consequence of the 1959 treaty that set one yard to 0.9144 meters.

The conversion is exact once you accept those treaty definitions. But in practice, it gets rounded —and the rounding direction depends on who benefits.

Rounding to 0.4 ha/acre —a common shortcut in informal international farmland listings —understates the land area by 1.16%. On a 5,000-acre farm listed at $8,000/acre ($40 million total), that's a $464,000 undervaluation of the asset. The buyer gets a discount they didn't negotiate for. The seller leaves money on the table. The broker's commission is calculated on the listed price, so the broker has no incentive to catch the error.

Rounding to 0.405 ha/acre —the standard agricultural rounding —is accurate to within 0.08%. On 1,000 acres, the error is 0.3 hectares —roughly the size of a suburban backyard. This is the safe rounding for agribusiness transactions, USDA reports, and real estate listing services. It is not sufficient for survey-grade work, but it is the industry-standard compromise between precision and readability.

The deliberate mislabel —a parcel listed as "500" without a unit, where the seller surveyed in acres and the contract was written by the seller's agent with "hectares" in the legal description —is not a rounding error. It is a factor-of-2.47 discrepancy. The buyer believes they are purchasing 500 hectares (1,236 acres). The seller knows the land is 500 acres (202 hectares). When the dispute reaches arbitration, the arbitrator examines the survey plat —which is in acres —and the contract —which states "hectares." The plat controls. The buyer paid for 1,236 acres. They received 500. The difference is 736 acres —roughly $5.9 million at Iowa farmland prices, and more than the legal cost of litigating it. The buyer's due diligence should have caught it. The buyer's local surveyor, hired after the contract was signed, flagged the discrepancy. But the contract had already been executed, and the arbitration clause specified the seller's jurisdiction. This exact fact pattern has appeared in agricultural land arbitrations in South America, Eastern Europe, and Southeast Asia, where foreign investors buy farmland in jurisdictions with limited title insurance and survey-data transparency.

Carbon Credits: The New Frontier of the Acre-Hectare Problem

The global carbon offset market —valued at roughly $2 billion in 2023 —requires project areas to be registered in hectares. The Verified Carbon Standard (Verra, VCS) and the Gold Standard both use hectares as the unit of land area for forestry, agricultural soil carbon, and wetland restoration projects. A project developer in the United States surveys their forest in acres. An auditor in Amsterdam reviews the project documentation in hectares. The conversion sits between them.

A reforestation project on 10,000 acres of degraded pasture in the Mississippi Delta —if converted with the rounded factor of 0.4 ha/acre —registers at 4,000 hectares. The correct area: 4,046.9 hectares. The 46.9-hectare gap, if planted at 1,100 trees per hectare and modeled at 15 tonnes CO—per hectare per year over a 30-year crediting period, represents roughly 21,000 tonnes of uncounted carbon —approximately $630,000 in uncounted credits at a $30/tonne carbon price. Over the full 30-year project lifetime, the rounding decision compounds into a seven-figure revenue gap.

Carbon auditors now cite the conversion factor explicitly in project design documents. The Verra VCS standard's template for AFOLU (Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use) projects includes a field for "Conversion factor used (acres to hectares)" —an acknowledgement that the error is common enough to warrant an explicit check. A project that fails to state its conversion factor is flagged during validation. A project that uses the wrong conversion factor —and has credits issued on the wrong area —faces a buffer withholding or, in extreme cases, a reversal of issued credits. The acre-to-hectare conversion, once the casual business of a real estate agent with a pocket calculator, is now a line item on a carbon auditor's checklist.

Engineering Context

Civil engineering inherited the acre in hydrology and stormwater management, where the Rational Method —Q = CiA —expects A in acres in US practice and hectares everywhere else. But converting A alone is not enough. The rainfall intensity i changes from inches per hour to mm per hour (multiply by 25.4). The runoff coefficient C is dimensionless and stays the same —one merciful constant. The discharge Q flips from cubic feet per second to liters per second or cubic meters per second (multiply by 28.317 for ft³/s to L/s). A detention basin designed for a 100-year storm event using the wrong combination of units will be dimensionally inconsistent —and the 100-year storm will overtop it. Civil engineers in the US learn the Rational Method in US units in school and encounter the metric version for the first time on an international project. The conversion is taught as a footnote. The consequence —a flooded subdivision or an undersized culvert —is the exam question that the footnote doesn't cover. See the Length Conversion Guide for the foot-to-meter and inch-to-millimeter conversions that underpin area conversions. For the reverse direction, hectares to acres divides by the same factor. At the smaller end of the area spectrum, acres to sq ft (1 ac = 43,560 ft²) and sq ft to sq m (1 ft² = 0.09290304 m²) handle architectural and construction-scale conversions. For agricultural yield comparisons —bushels per acre on one side of the Atlantic, tonnes per hectare on the other —both the area and the mass must convert simultaneously, or the yield comparison is meaningless. A 200 bu/ac corn yield converts to 12.55 tonnes/ha assuming 56 lb/bu and standard 15.5% moisture. A European farmer reading "200 bu/ac" and mentally comparing it to 200 tonnes/ha is making an order-of-magnitude error.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the acre still exist when hectares are clearly more logical?

The acre survives for the same reason the mile and the gallon survive: institutional inertia, legal precedent, and the cost of switching. Every property deed, every tax assessment, every agricultural subsidy formula, every real estate listing in the United States is denominated in acres. Converting the US land title system to hectares would require rewriting approximately 2.4 billion pages of county recorder documents across 3,144 counties and county-equivalents. The cost of that conversion would be borne by the public sector. The benefit —a clean decimal unit —accrues mainly to international investors and carbon registries. The political math has never added up. The acre will persist until the cost of maintaining two systems exceeds the cost of picking one. That day is not close.

How do I verify the acre-to-hectare conversion on a land contract?

Multiply the stated acres by 0.40468564224. Multiply the stated hectares by 2.47105381467. If both numbers appear on the contract and the cross-multiplication doesn't match, something was rounded. If only one unit appears, ask for the other in writing. If the contract says "500" without a unit, do not sign it until the unit is stated explicitly. This is not pedantry. A contract for "500" of land that the seller later claims was surveyed in acres while the buyer understood hectares is an enforceable ambiguity, and courts and arbitrators resolve ambiguity against the party that drafted the contract. If you are the buyer, do not let the seller's agent draft the unit description. Write it in yourself: "500 acres (202.34 hectares)." The five seconds it takes to type those parentheses have resolved disputes worth millions.

Are acres used differently in different countries besides the US?

The United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and India all retain the acre in varying degrees. The UK still uses acres for agricultural land (the hectare is official, but farmers and estate agents use acres). Canada operates in a hybrid: the hectare is official for government reporting, but farmland is bought and sold in acres, and the quarter-section —160 acres —is the cultural unit of Prairie agriculture. Australia metricated in the 1970s but rural land is still discussed in acres colloquially —"a thousand-acre spread" sounds better in a real estate ad than "405 hectares." India uses acres for land records in some states and hectares in others, with the additional complication of local units like the bigha, which varies in size from state to state. Ireland switched entirely to hectares for land registration in the 1980s but the acre survives in conversation. The acre is like the pint —metricated on paper, alive in speech.

What's the difference between a US survey acre and an international acre?

The acre itself is always 43,560 square feet. The difference was in which foot was used to define the square foot. Before 2023, the US had two feet: the international foot (0.3048 m) and the US survey foot (1,200/3,937 m —0.30480061 m). An acre expressed in international square feet was 4,046.8564224 m². An acre expressed in US survey square feet was 4,046.8726099 m² —a difference of 0.016 m² per acre, or about 16 square centimeters. Across a 640-acre section, the difference was 10.4 m² —the size of a small bedroom. The US survey foot was deprecated on January 1, 2023 by NIST and NOAA. All federal surveying now uses the international foot. The acre-to-hectare conversion factor on this page uses the international foot. If you are working with pre-2023 US survey data at the scale of an entire state, verify which foot was used in the original control network. For a single parcel of land, the 16 cm² per acre difference is smaller than the uncertainty of the survey instrument that measured it.

How do I quickly estimate acres to hectares?

Multiply by 0.4 and add 1.2% of the result. 100 acres: 100 × 0.4 = 40, plus 0.48 = 40.48 ha. Actual: 40.47 ha. Error: 0.01 ha —the size of a parking space. For rough mental math, multiplying acres by 0.4 gets you within 1.2% —close enough for "how big is this ranch" and not close enough for a contract. For hectares to acres: multiply by 2.5 and subtract 1.2%. 100 ha: 100 × 2.5 = 250, minus 3 = 247 acres. Actual: 247.1 acres. Same gap, reversed.
