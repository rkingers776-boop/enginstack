Two Farms, Two Units, One Global Market

A wheat farmer in Saskatchewan measures their land in acres — 2,000 of them, roughly 809 hectares. A wheat farmer in France measures theirs in hectares — 150 of them, roughly 371 acres. Both sell their grain on the Chicago Board of Trade, where futures contracts are priced in US dollars per bushel and yield is reported in bushels per acre. The French farmer converts their yield from quintals per hectare (the European standard) to bushels per acre to compare with the Chicago price. The conversion chain — hectares to acres, quintals to bushels, euros to dollars — runs through every global grain trade. One hectare equals 2.47105 acres, and that number is stamped into the workflow of every agricultural economist, every commodity trader, and every farmer who has ever wondered how their yield compares to the global average.

Why the Hectare Makes More Sense Than the Acre

acres = hectares × 2.47105381467165

The hectare was designed by the French Revolution's metric reformers: a square 100 meters on each side, yielding exactly 10,000 m². No fractions, no chains, no oxen. The acre inherited 43,560 — a number that made sense to medieval surveyors with 66-foot chains but makes no sense to anyone else. The hectare-to-acre conversion factor (≈2.471) is awkward precisely because one unit was designed for logical consistency and the other was inherited from agricultural tradition. There's no clean ratio between them because they were never meant to coexist.

What the Conversion Means for Global Food

Crop yield is the universal metric of agricultural productivity, and it's where the hectare-acre divide creates constant arithmetic friction. The global corn yield average is approximately 5.8 tonnes per hectare, or 92.4 bushels per acre. The conversion requires three separate factors: hectares to acres (×2.471), tonnes to bushels (for corn, ×39.368), and the yield calculation itself. The USDA reports US corn yield in bushels per acre; the FAO reports global yield in tonnes per hectare. Analysts who compare these numbers daily develop an intuitive feel for the conversion — "about 2.5 acres to the hectare, about 40 bushels per tonne" — but the compounding of multiple conversions creates a margin of imprecision that makes cross-border agricultural statistics inherently approximate.

Land valuation follows the same pattern. Farmland in Iowa sells for approximately $12,000 per acre (2025 average). The equivalent in Île-de-France is about €8,000 per hectare. Converting: $12,000/acre × 2.471 acres/hectare = $29,652/hectare, then convert to euros at the prevailing exchange rate. A 10% shift in the currency cross rate changes the apparent land value more than the hectare-to-acre conversion error ever could — which is why international land investors pay more attention to forex than to unit conversion. But the conversion is still the first step in the spreadsheet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why doesn't the world just pick one — hectares or acres — and standardize?

Because land records are the most politically entrenched documents in any country. The United States has approximately 1.5 billion acres of deeded land, all surveyed and titled in acres. Converting the entire country's land registry to hectares would cost billions of dollars and trigger a century of boundary disputes as rounding errors accumulated across property lines. The UK, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand face the same issue. Meanwhile, the 180+ countries that use the metric system have already standardized on hectares for land measurement. The result is a permanent coexistence, like driving on the left vs the right side of the road: switching would cause more problems than it solves. For cross-border transactions, convert. For domestic transactions, use the local unit. The conversion factor is permanent.

How do international environmental agreements handle the hectare-acre divide?

They standardize on hectares. The UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, the Convention on Biological Diversity, and the FAO's Global Forest Resources Assessment all report land area in hectares. When the Brazilian government reports Amazon deforestation of 10,000 km² per year, that's 1,000,000 hectares (100 × 100 km) — or 2,471,000 acres. US media outlets typically convert to acres for domestic audiences because the number is more meaningful to American readers. The conversion factor is always the same: multiply hectares by 2.471 to get acres. The environmental policy is hectares. The American news report is acres. The planet's forest cover doesn't care which unit you use.

What's the relationship between a hectare and a soccer field?

A standard FIFA soccer pitch is 105 m × 68 m = 7,140 m², or 0.714 hectares (1.76 acres). So one hectare is about 1.4 soccer pitches — a convenient visualization for urban audiences who don't regularly walk across farmland. For Americans: one acre is about 0.9 football fields (an NFL field is 360 ft × 160 ft = 57,600 sq ft = 1.32 acres). Neither analogy is exact, but both are close enough to make the numbers meaningful to non-farmers. The soccer-field hectare (1.4 pitches per hectare) and the football-field acre (0.76 fields per acre) are the two most common land-area visualizations in the world.
