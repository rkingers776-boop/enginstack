How to Convert km/L to US MPG

Converting kilometers per liter to US miles per gallon is essential for reading Asian-market car specifications through an American lens —it's how you discover that a Honda Civic rated 15.6 km/L in Japan equals 36.7 MPG in US terms, how a Korean-market Hyundai Tucson at 14 km/L translates to 32.9 MPG for an American reviewer, and how Indian-market Maruti Suzukis that advertise 22 km/L are achieving an extraordinary 51.7 MPG. 1 km/L = 2.35215 US MPG. Multiply km/L by 2.352. This conversion uses the US gallon (3.78541 L). The conversion factor derives from 1 km = 0.621371 miles and 1 L = 0.264172 US gallons, so 1 km/L = 0.621371 / 0.264172 = 2.35215 miles/US gallon.

The Conversion Formula

US MPG = km/L × 2.35215

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert km/L to US MPG?

Multiply km/L by 2.35215. Formula: US MPG = km/L × 2.35215. For a quick estimate, multiply by 2.35 —the error is less than 0.1 MPG for typical values (10— km/L).

Which countries use km/L for fuel economy?

Japan (JC08/WLTC cycles), South Korea, India (ARAI certifications), Thailand, Indonesia, and most of Southeast Asia display fuel economy in km/L. Brazil uses km/L for ethanol and gasoline. China uses L/100km officially but many consumer sites show km/L as a secondary metric. Combined, these markets represent approximately 50 million new vehicle sales per year —roughly 60% of global production.

Is a higher km/L better?

Yes —km/L is a "distance per fuel" metric, so higher is better, like MPG. This aligns with consumer intuition: "I go farther on a liter." L/100km is the inverse —I consume less per 100 km" —and lower is better. The human factors research (EPA Green Book, 2010) that led to the US adding gallons/100 miles confirmed that consumers make more accurate cost-saving decisions with "per-distance" metrics because fuel savings are linear in L/100km but hyperbolic in km/L.

Engineering Context

The km/L-to-MPG conversion is the default translation layer in every global automotive benchmarking database. When Toyota's Higashi-Fuji Technical Center tests a new Camry powertrain on the JC08 dynamometer cycle, the raw result is 15.6 km/L —but the same data appears as 36.7 MPG in Toyota Motor North America's Plano headquarters and as 6.41 L/100km in Toyota Motor Europe's Brussels office. All three numbers are generated by the same CAN bus fuel-flow sensor (a Horiba FQ-2100DP ultrasonic flow meter with ±1% accuracy) with the same 0.1 mL resolution. The AECS (Automotive Energy Consumption Simulator) used by suppliers like Bosch and Denso for EPA pre-certification runs a full vehicle model in Simulink and outputs fuel economy in all three units simultaneously —the km/L-to-MPG conversion constant 2.352145 is a #define in the AUTOSAR application layer, and a mismatch between the OEM's #define and the supplier's #define has caused at least three documented stop-ship incidents when the EPA label value didn't match the certification submission. In the used-car export trade, Japanese auction sheets (the USS Tokyo auction house handles 15,000 vehicles per week) list fuel economy in km/L per the JC08 test —but a UAE or Kenyan buyer reads 12 km/L on the sheet and must mentally convert to 28.2 MPG or 8.3 km/L (both common in different English-speaking import markets). One dealer in Mombasa imports 200 JDM vehicles per month and employs two full-time data entry clerks whose sole job is converting auction-sheet specifications to local units —the km/L-to-MPG ratio 2.35215 is written on a Post-it note taped to every monitor in the office. In climate policy, the conversion is statutory: New Zealand's Clean Car Standard (2023) sets CO—targets in g/km, but imported used vehicles from Japan arrive with fuel-economy labels in km/L. The NZ Transport Agency's conversion from km/L to g CO—km uses: g CO—km = (2,320 g/L for petrol) / (km/L × 1,000) × 1,000 = 2,320 / km/L. A 15.6 km/L car = 148.7 g CO—km —just under the 150 g/km penalty threshold that triggers an NZ$2,300 fee. The single conversion 2.35215 thus determines whether a NZ$15,000 JDM import is affordable or uneconomical for a Christchurch family.
