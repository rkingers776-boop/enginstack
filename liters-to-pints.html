Liters to Pints: A 330 mL Beer Bottle Is 0.70 US Pints —the Conversion That Runs Through Every Import Beer Drinker's Head

A Belgian tripel arrives at the table in a 330 mL bottle. The American next to you asks, "How much is that in pints?" It's 0.70 US pints —not even three-quarters. The Brit across the table does the same math in Imperial: 0.58 UK pints, just over half. Everyone at the table is converting liters to pints. Nobody is happy about it. The liter-to-pint step is one of the most frequently performed mental conversions in bars, restaurants, and kitchens worldwide —because beer is metric in Europe and served by the pint everywhere else, and because recipes written in liters constantly need to be mapped onto American pint containers. The two systems meet in the pint glass.

The Crowned Pint Glass and the 2008 EU Revolt

In 2008, the European Commission proposed a directive that would have ended the mandatory use of Imperial markings on UK pint glasses. The crown stamp —the government certification mark that guarantees a pub glass holds exactly one Imperial pint —would have become optional. British tabloids ran headlines like "EU Bans the Pint" and "They Can't Take Our Crown." The Commission wasn't banning the pint; it was proposing that British pubs could also use metric-marked glasses without the crown stamp. But the political interpretation was that Brussels was coming for the pint. The British government secured a permanent exemption. The crowned pint glass remains a legal requirement for draught beer in the UK. The symbolism matters more than the volume: the pint's survival as a legal unit is a visible reminder of national sovereignty in a country that has metricated almost everything else. For the history of why the UK pint diverged from the US pint in 1824, see the companion page: pints to liters.

The Metric Beer Bottle and the Pint Glass: A Design Mismatch

A standard US pint glass holds 16 fl oz (473 mL). A standard European beer bottle holds 330 mL or 500 mL. Neither fits a pint glass cleanly. Pour a 330 mL bottle into a US pint glass and the glass is 70% full —the head fills the remaining 30% if you pour aggressively, but a gentle pour leaves a disappointing inch of empty glass. Pour a 500 mL bottle into the same glass and it overflows. Pour it into a UK Imperial pint glass (568 mL) and the beer fills 88% —enough room for a small head, a neat visual compromise. The 500 mL bottle is arguably designed for the Imperial pint glass. The 330 mL bottle is designed for the 250 mL European beer glass (the "half-liter" bottle's smaller sibling). None of these volumes align with the US pint. The global beer industry has never harmonized its glassware with its bottle sizes, because doing so would require either America to abandon the 16 fl oz pint (politically impossible) or Europe to bottle beer in 473 mL increments (a retooling cost no brewery would absorb). The pint glass and the metric bottle will remain mismatched forever.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pints in a 2-liter bottle of soda?

2 liters = 4.227 US pints = 3.520 UK pints. A US 2-liter soda bottle holds slightly more than 4 US pints (half a US gallon —which is exactly 4 pints —plus an extra 0.227 pints, about 3.6 fl oz). So a 2-liter of Coke is about 67.6 fl oz, while a US half-gallon is exactly 64 fl oz. The 2-liter is larger than the half-gallon it replaced on the supermarket shelf. This is the rare case of metrication giving the consumer more product —the 3.6 extra ounces cost the manufacturer less than the retooling cost to shrink the bottle to exactly 64 oz. For the reverse math and more carbonated-beverage history, see liters to quarts and the Volume & Cooking Guide.

Can I approximate 1 liter as 2 pints for cooking?

For soups, stews, and stocks: yes. 1 L ≈2 US pints + 3.6 fl oz, and the extra half-cup of liquid will be absorbed by the recipe's built-in tolerance for stock quantity (soup recipes are never exact —the "add more stock if too thick" instruction absorbs the error). For baking liquids (milk in a cake, water in bread dough): no. The extra 3.6 fl oz per liter is a 5.7% overpour —enough to shift the dough hydration from 65% to 69%, which changes the crumb. For cocktails: no. A recipe calling for "500 mL of tonic water" split among 4 glasses is 125 mL per glass. Rounding to 1 US pint (473 mL) and splitting it 4 ways gives 118 mL per glass —a 5.6% shortfall per drink. For homebrew: absolutely not. A 5% volume error in a 5-gallon batch is a quart —enough to shift the original gravity by several points. Homebrewers should use a graduated vessel marked in liters and ignore pints entirely. Engineering Context

1 L = 2.11337642 US pt = 1.75975326 UK pt. These conversions derive from the gallon definitions: US gallon = 231 in³ = 3.785411784 L →1 US pt = 0.473176473 L →1 L = 2.11337642 US pt. UK gallon = 4.54609 L →1 UK pt = 0.56826125 L →1 L = 1.75975326 UK pt. In plant engineering, the liter-to-pint step matters when a European reactor vessel is sized in liters (e.g., a 500 L batch reactor) and the US process piping is rated in gallons per minute —which requires converting throughput in liters per batch to pints per minute through the pipe. The engineering move is to take everything to liters, convert the pipe rating to L/min, and leave the pints out of the control system entirely. The pint is an operator-facing unit; the liter is the control-system unit. For the reverse: pints to liters. Adjacent: liters to quarts, liters to gallons, mL to cups.
