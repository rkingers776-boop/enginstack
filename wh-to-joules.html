The 3,600 That Connects Batteries to Physics

Energy is energy, whether a battery label calls it watt hours or a textbook calls it joules. The watt hour is defined through the joule: one watt is one joule per second, so one watt sustained for 3,600 seconds (one hour) is exactly 3,600 joules. That makes the conversion a definition, not a measurement — and it is why the numbers get big fast. A phone battery at 15 Wh is 54,000 J; an e-bike pack at 500 Wh is 1.8 MJ; a home battery at 13.5 kWh is 48.6 MJ. The joule is the SI unit every downstream calculation — thermodynamics, mechanical work, heat — expects, and this page supplies it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert watt hours to joules?

Multiply by 3,600: joules = watt hours × 3,600. A 500 Wh e-bike battery is 500 × 3,600 = 1,800,000 J = 1.8 MJ. The factor is exact: a watt is one joule per second, and an hour is 3,600 seconds, so 1 Wh = 1 W × 3,600 s = 3,600 J.

Why do batteries use watt hours instead of joules?

Because watt hours connect directly to the power ratings people actually use — a 100 W device draws 100 Wh in an hour, so a 500 Wh battery runs it for five hours. Joules are the SI energy unit but produce unmanageably large numbers at battery scale (500 Wh = 1.8 million J). Both are the same energy; the watt hour is the consumer-friendly name, the joule is the physics unit.

How many joules are in a kilowatt hour?

Exactly 3,600,000 J = 3.6 MJ, because 1 kWh = 1,000 Wh and each Wh is 3,600 J. This is the electricity-bill number in physics units: a typical US home uses about 30 kWh per day, which is 108 MJ — the energy of about 26 kg of gasoline burned. The kWh-to-joules conversion is the same factor scaled by 1,000.
