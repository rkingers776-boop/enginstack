The Unit Physics Actually Runs On

Every formula in dynamics, fluid mechanics, and ballistics expects velocity in meters per second — acceleration in m/s², energy in joules, Reynolds numbers dimensionless against m/s. The imperial world's answer is ft/s, the matching unit of the foot-pound-second system, and the two are locked together by the 1959 definition of the foot. Multiply by 0.3048 and a US wind-tunnel data sheet becomes a SI CFD input without losing a digit of meaning. This is the quiet conversion that runs inside every international engineering project: the aircraft performance table in ft/s, the runway certification in m/s, the same number twice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert ft/s to m/s?

Multiply by 0.3048: m/s = ft/s × 0.3048. 88 ft/s (60 mph) is 88 × 0.3048 = 26.82 m/s. The factor is exact because the foot is defined as 0.3048 meters by the 1959 agreement.

Why do engineers use ft/s and m/s instead of mph and km/h?

Because physics formulas are built on meters and seconds — acceleration in m/s², force in newtons, flow in m³/s — and ft/s is the imperial system's matching unit. mph and km/h mix hours into the denominator, which breaks most equations. A wind-tunnel spec of 26.82 m/s and an aircraft data sheet of 88 ft/s are the same velocity; engineers pick the unit that fits their formula system.

What is 60 mph in ft/s and m/s?

60 mph is exactly 88 ft/s — a conversion every US driver's-ed student learns — and 88 ft/s is 26.82 m/s. The 88 comes from 60 miles × 5,280 ft ÷ 3,600 seconds. From there, every car, train, and aircraft speed can be stepped through the same two-factor chain.
