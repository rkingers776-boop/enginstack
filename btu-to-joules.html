BTU to joules — the HVAC nameplate, translated into science

An air conditioner is "9,000 BTU," a furnace "60,000 BTU," and a water heater "40,000 BTU" — and none of those numbers is in joules, the unit every physics textbook and SI regulation uses. The translation is exact: 1 BTU = 1,055.05585262 J. A 9,000 BTU AC moves 9.5 MJ of heat every hour. Two Industries, One Energy

In 1845, James Prescott Joule measured how much mechanical work makes heat; in the same era, American furnace builders defined the BTU as the heat needed to warm one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. Both were measuring the same thing — energy — but the two definitions never merged. A century and a half later, a building's heating load is still quoted in BTU while every SI-based calculation, every government efficiency regulation, and every physics course works in joules. The conversion factor that connects them is exact because both units are definitions: 1 BTU (IT) = 1,055.05585262 J, and the second is the SI unit, so nothing about this conversion involves measurement uncertainty.

Read the units in that table carefully — every one says BTU per hour, and every one becomes joules per hour, not joules. The nameplate "9,000 BTU" is shorthand for 9,000 BTU per hour; the energy it moves in a full day is 9.5 MJ × 24 = 228 MJ, and conflating the two numbers is how undersized HVAC gets speced. When you see joules in an efficiency standard (SEER, EER, AFUE), the underlying arithmetic is exactly this conversion with time already folded in.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many joules are in one BTU?

Exactly 1,055.05585262 joules for the international table (IT) BTU defined by ISO 31-4. That is about 1.055 kJ — so 10,000 BTU is 10.55 MJ. The factor is a definition, not a measurement, so it carries no uncertainty.

What is the difference between BTU and BTU per hour?

BTU is an energy amount; BTU/h is a power — energy per unit time. An air conditioner 'rated 9,000 BTU' actually means 9,000 BTU per hour, which is 9.5 MJ per hour or about 2.64 kW of heat moved. Mixing the two is the classic HVAC error: the energy in joules needs the time included, while the nameplate number is already a rate.

Why does HVAC use BTU while science uses joules?

History: the BTU was defined in the 19th century as the heat to raise one pound of water one degree Fahrenheit, and the US HVAC industry grew up with it. The SI system kept the joule, defined mechanically. The conversion is exact, but the two worlds never converged — so engineers still translate, and this converter is that translation.
