1,609.344

Nobody remembers that number. Everybody remembers "1.6 kilometers to a mile." That's close enough to navigate. It's not close enough to certify a marathon course. The exact number — 1,609.344 — was typed into a treaty on July 1, 1959, along with its siblings: 1 yard = 0.9144 meter, 1 pound = 0.45359237 kilogram. Six countries signed. The US. The UK. Canada. Australia. New Zealand. South Africa. Before that day, an American inch was 25.4000508 millimeters and a British inch was about 25.399956 millimeters. The difference was two parts per million. Not enough to mis-cut a two-by-four. Enough to make a set of American gauge blocks incompatible with a British micrometer. After July 1, 1959, the inch was 25.4 millimeters everywhere. The mile became 1,609.344 meters everywhere. Americans and Brits could finally build things together without wondering which inch they were using.

The chain of definitions runs like this. In 1959, six nations signed a document that said: 1 yard = 0.9144 meter exactly. From that one sentence, everything followed. A foot is one-third of a yard: 0.3048 meter. An inch is one-thirty-sixth of a yard: 25.4 millimeters. A mile is 1,760 yards — a number Elizabeth I's Parliament fixed in 1593 — so a mile is 1,760 × 0.9144 = 1,609.344 meters. The mile had been defined in yards since 1593. The yard had been defined in meters since 1959. The meter has been defined by the speed of light since 1983. So the mile is now, indirectly, defined by the speed of light. A photon in vacuum covers a mile in about 5.37 microseconds. That's what the mile is now: a time interval.

The US kept a ghost. The old Mendenhall foot — the one that made a mile roughly 1,609.3472 meters — was retained for surveying. All the State Plane Coordinate maps, all the Public Land Survey monuments, all the deeds written between 1893 and 1959 used the old foot. Retracting every property boundary in the country to the new foot would have cost billions and broken every deed in the chain of title. So the US had two feet, and therefore two miles, from 1959 until January 1, 2023 — 64 years. On that date, the National Geodetic Survey officially retired the US survey foot. One mile now. 1,609.344 meters. Everywhere.

Multiply miles by 1,609.344 to get meters. One mile is roughly 1,600 meters — the approximation runners use. A 5K (5,000 meters) is 3.10686 miles. A half marathon is 21,097.5 meters = 13.10938 miles. A marathon — 42,195 flat — is 26.21875 miles. Track athletes who run the mile in competition are running 1,609.344 meters, not 1,600. That extra 9.344 meters is 4.3 seconds at elite pace — the gap between a good race and a great one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert miles to meters?

Multiply by 1,609.344. meters = miles × 1,609.344. If you're doing it in your head, 1 mile ≈ 1,600 meters is your starting point — it's about 0.6% low. For anything that matters (race timing, survey closures, engineering takeoffs), use the exact number.

Is a mile longer than 1,500 meters?

Yes. 1,500 meters is 0.932 miles. The Olympic 1,500 m is nicknamed the metric mile. It's 109 meters short. That's the length of a football field. At elite pace, covering those extra 109 meters takes about 15 seconds. That's why a 3:50 mile is harder than a 3:35 1,500 — same runner, same effort, but the mile makes you hold it for 109 more meters.

Engineering Context

Most international engineering work uses meters. American road designers use miles for route-level planning and feet for detailed design. When a European guardrail system gets installed on a US highway, the spec sheet is in meters, the plans are in feet, and the conversion (×1,609.344 or ÷1,609.344) happens at least three times: once in the bid estimate, once in the shop drawings, once in the as-built survey. Every time, someone types a number into a calculator. Two of those three times, they probably get it right. Length Conversion Guide.
