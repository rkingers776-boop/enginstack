How to Convert Cm to Yd

The global textile trade runs on this conversion. European fabric is woven to metric widths: 150 cm is the standard bolt width for cotton shirting, wool suiting, and linen yardage across the continent. A US buyer sees "59 inches" on the spec sheet — which is actually 149.86 cm, not 150. That 0.14 cm difference looks trivial on paper. Across a 5,000-yard production run, it compounds into real yardage variance. Procurement managers in Los Angeles and New York stare at the same number every morning: a centimeter spec from a mill in Biella or Prato, waiting to be divided by 91.44.

Inside the apparel supply chain, the conversion lives on the cutting room floor. A Chinese factory receives a US spec calling for 2.5 yards of 150 cm-wide fabric per jacket. The cutting table is calibrated in centimeters. 2.5 yards equals 228.6 cm. The fabric width stays at 150 cm. The cutter lays out pattern pieces within a 150 by 228.6 cm rectangle — one axis pure metric, the other converted from a US yardage spec. Every jacket lining, every pocket facing, every collar piece nests inside that hybrid-dimension boundary. The operator doesn't think about the conversion. The CAD file embedded it.

The Vietnam-Bangladesh garment corridor handles the bulk of global apparel production. Patterns originate in the US or UK, designed in inches and yards. Production happens in metric countries. The cm-to-yard conversion fires at the CAD nesting stage, when Gerber and Lectra cutting machines optimize fabric usage. Both machine families accept either unit system — but the operator must select the correct one before loading the cut file. A 2019 internal audit at a Vietnam factory found that 12% of cutting orders had unit selection errors flagged during pre-production checks. Twelve percent. Most were caught. Some weren't.

The yard itself is 91.44 cm. That .44 cm — 4.4 millimeters — is the gap between 91 cm and 91.44 cm. A tailor measuring a 91 cm length and ordering "one yard" is actually ordering 0.44 cm too little fabric. For a single garment, invisible. Across 100 yards, that's a 44 cm shortfall — nearly half a meter of missing material. The 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement fixed the yard at 0.9144 meters exactly. The .44 is not a rounding error. It's the definition. Drop it, and your error rate is 0.48% per yard. Procurement directors who ignore the decimal eventually explain a fabric shortage to their production manager.

European sewing patterns tell the same story from the consumer side. Burda, Ottobre, and other European pattern houses print all measurements in centimeters. A US sewist downloading a PDF pattern from a German website sees fabric requirements like "320 cm of 140 cm-wide fabric." That's 3.5 yards of 55-inch-wide fabric. Simple enough. But US fabric comes in 44-45 inch or 54-60 inch widths — neither matches the European 140 cm (55 inches) exactly. The width conversion is its own problem. The sewist must check whether the narrower US fabric will fit the pattern pieces before buying yardage. One conversion cascades into a second.

The Conversion Formula

yd = cm ÷ 91.44  or  yd = cm × 0.01093613

Worked Examples

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert centimeters to yards?

Divide the number of centimeters by 91.44. Formula: yards = cm ÷ 91.44. Alternatively, multiply by 0.01093613.

How do I buy US fabric when my measurements are in cm?

Divide your centimeter measurement by 91.44 to get the yardage. Example: you need 300 cm of fabric — 300 ÷ 91.44 = 3.28 yards. Purchase 3.5 yards to ensure you have enough.

Is 100 cm exactly 1 meter, and how does that relate to yards?

100 cm = 1 m = 1.0936 yards. A meter is about 9.4% longer than a yard — so when converting cm to yards, the yard number is always slightly smaller than the meter number.

Engineering Context

Centimeters-to-yards (1 cm = 0.010936 yd) is the gateway conversion for metric-world manufacturers entering US textile and construction supply chains. A European technical fabric spec listing a roll width of 150 cm must be communicated to US buyers as 1.641 yards. In international sporting goods manufacturing, a German-made soccer goal net measured at 732 cm wide × 244 cm tall converts to 8.00 yd × 2.67 yd — the yard numbers look irregular but exactly match the metric spec. In global apparel, a Chinese size chart listing a 104 cm chest converts to a 40.94-inch chest (US size 40R), but the fabric consumption calculation for production planning must be in yards: a jacket requiring 250 cm of 150 cm wide fabric uses 2.73 yards. Getting the cm-to-yd conversion wrong at the procurement stage either wastes fabric or causes production shortfall.
