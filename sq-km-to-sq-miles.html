Square Kilometers to Square Miles: The Metric Atlas Reading an Imperial Map

1 km² = 0.386102 sq mi. Open Wikipedia to any country article and the area is given in square kilometers — always. Open any US newspaper and the same territory appears in square miles. The conversion bridges two audiences that describe the same planet in different units, and the number 0.3861021585 is the key. It is the only number that turns a European Union land-use report into something a Nebraska county commissioner can visualize. It translates a Siberian wildfire that burned 500 km² into a 193 sq mi headline for readers who have never seen a kilometer. It is why every GIS analyst who works with global datasets keeps this factor bookmarked, and why anyone reading about deforestation in the Amazon or glacier retreat in Greenland ends up doing this conversion, whether they realize it or not.

Why Square Kilometers Rule the World

The world's land area is described in square kilometers. Not by accident — by design. The United Nations Statistics Division uses km². The World Bank's World Development Indicators use km². The CIA World Factbook uses km². Eurostat's Land Cover/Use statistics use km² and hectares. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change reports ice-sheet loss in km². NASA's Earth Observatory publishes deforestation rates in km² per year. The Global Forest Watch platform tracks tree-cover loss exclusively in hectares and square kilometers. If you want to participate in any conversation about global land use, you start in square kilometers — and then, if your audience is American, you convert.

The dominance of km² is a downstream consequence of the metric system's near-universal adoption. Every country except the United States, Liberia, and Myanmar uses metric units as its official measurement system. That is roughly 4.3% of the world's population that does not think natively in kilometers — and roughly 100% of the world's satellite data, climate models, and land-cover classifications that output results in them. The asymmetry is complete: the data speaks metric, and the American reader speaks imperial. The conversion is not optional; it is the cost of entry.

This is why Wikipedia's country infoboxes list area in km² first, with a parenthetical in square miles. The convention was settled by community consensus in the mid-2000s and has held ever since, surviving dozens of talk-page battles between editors who wanted mi² first for American readers and those who pointed out that the rest of the world edits Wikipedia too. The compromise — metric primary, imperial in parentheses — works because the math is reversible. To go the other direction, see the companion page: square miles to square kilometers.

Reading the Global Atlas Through American Eyes

Every country comparison in a US newsroom starts the same way: a reporter looks up the country on Wikipedia, sees the area in km², and reaches for the calculator. Ethiopia: 1,104,300 km². Divide by 2.59. Result: 426,400 sq mi — slightly larger than Texas and Oklahoma combined. Bolivia: 1,098,581 km² — 424,200 sq mi, marginally smaller than Ethiopia, a fact lost on most readers until the numbers sit side by side in the same unit. France (European portion): 551,695 km² — 213,000 sq mi, roughly the combined landmass of Colorado and Wyoming. The conversion is a translation layer between the metric database that the internet runs on and the imperial mental models that American readers carry.

This translation layer is surprisingly thin. The factor 0.386102 is compact enough for mental arithmetic if you round to 0.39: multiply km² by 0.4 and subtract 2.5% for a quick estimate. A 10,000 km² lake becomes roughly 3,900 sq mi with the shortcut; the precise answer is 3,861 sq mi, an error of 39 sq mi — about the size of Disney World — and acceptable for cocktail-party geography. For journalism, tables, and international comparisons, use the exact factor. For the editor on deadline, 0.39 gets you close enough to write the sentence and move on.

When the News Burned 500 Square Kilometers

Wildfire reporting is where the km²-to-sq-mi conversion becomes visceral. In July 2024, the Jasper wildfire complex in Alberta burned approximately 32,000 hectares — 320 km², or 124 sq mi. The Park Fire in California, three weeks later, consumed 429,000 acres — roughly 1,736 km², or 670 sq mi. Canadian and European outlets reported both fires in hectares and square kilometers. American outlets converted everything to acres and square miles. The same fire, described in different units, reads as two different disasters until you do the math.

The conversion becomes even more critical when comparing fire seasons across continents. Australia's 2019–2020 bushfire season burned an estimated 186,000 km² (71,800 sq mi) — an area larger than Florida. Siberia's 2021 fire season consumed roughly 183,000 km² (70,700 sq mi), predominantly in Sakha Republic taiga. The 2020 California fire season, by comparison, burned 17,000 km² (6,600 sq mi) — devastating by any measure, but roughly one-tenth of either Australian or Siberian totals. These comparisons only make sense when the units match, and the units only match when someone converts. For smaller fire footprint comparisons, see acres to square feet and acres to hectares.

Parks Without Borders: How Many Square Miles Is Your Favorite National Park?

National park comparison is a hobby that requires unit conversion. The world's largest national park is Northeast Greenland National Park at 972,000 km² (375,300 sq mi) — larger than all but 29 countries. Wrangell-St. Elias in Alaska covers 53,321 km² (20,587 sq mi), the largest in the US system. Canada's Wood Buffalo National Park is 44,807 km² (17,300 sq mi). Tanzania's Serengeti is 14,763 km² (5,700 sq mi). Kruger National Park in South Africa: 19,485 km² (7,523 sq mi). Every single one of these numbers was originally measured and published in square kilometers by the managing government. The UNESCO World Heritage database, which catalogs many of these parks, uses hectares exclusively — 1,000 hectares = 10 km² = 3.86 sq mi.

The US National Park Service, despite being an American agency, publishes official park areas in both acres and square kilometers. The dual reporting is not bureaucratic overkill — it is recognition that park science is international. Researchers comparing grizzly bear habitat density in Yellowstone (8,992 km² / 3,472 sq mi) with brown bear ranges in Kamchatka (roughly 470,000 km² / 181,500 sq mi) need consistent units to calculate densities, territory overlap, and carrying capacity. The raw data comes in km². The American park ranger's mental map is in square miles. Both can be right if the conversion is done correctly. And when it is not, the error propagates through ecological models that downstream researchers may never audit.

From Brussels to Washington: EU Land Data in American Terms

European Union land-use statistics are published by Eurostat exclusively in square kilometers and hectares. The CORINE Land Cover inventory, updated every six years, classifies 44 land-cover types across 39 European countries at a resolution of 25 hectares per minimum mapping unit. The total area tracked: roughly 5.8 million km² spanning from Portugal to Poland. Every category — artificial surfaces, arable land, permanent crops, pastures, forests, wetlands — is expressed in km². When an American policy think tank wants to compare EU agricultural land use to US farmland, the entire dataset must be converted to square miles or acres before side-by-side comparison is possible.

The conversion reveals structural differences that raw numbers conceal. France devotes roughly 290,000 km² (112,000 sq mi) to agricultural land — about 53% of its total area. The United States farms approximately 4.05 million km² (1.56 million sq mi) — about 44% of its land area. France farms a larger share of a much smaller country. In absolute square miles, the US farms 14 times more land than France, but French agricultural output per square kilometer is far higher due to climate, soil quality, and centuries of intensive cultivation. The raw km² numbers from Eurostat don't tell this story until they are converted and placed alongside USDA data in matching units. For smaller land parcel sizes, see hectares to acres and square feet to acres.

The Conversion: All You Need Is Division

The math is a single division because the forward conversion (sq mi to km²) uses multiplication by 2.589988110336. To reverse it: divide by that same number, or multiply by the reciprocal 0.3861021585. Both operations produce identical results because the underlying definition — 1 mile = 1609.344 meters, established by the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement — is exact. There is no rounding in the definition, only in how many decimal places you choose to display.

sq mi = km² ÷ 2.589988110336

or equivalently: sq mi = km² × 0.3861021585

Worked Examples

1 km² = 0.38610 sq mi

The base unit. About 247 acres, or roughly 100 city blocks at 2.5 acres per block. A square exactly 1 km on each side. If you stood at the center, the boundary would be 500 meters away in every direction — a 10-minute walk. One square kilometer is smaller than Central Park (3.41 km²) but larger than Vatican City (0.44 km²). In square miles, it is just over a third of a PLSS section.

500 km² burned in a wildfire = 193 sq mi

The standard unit of large-fire reporting in Canada and Europe. A 500 km² burn scar is roughly the area of the city of Denver (400 km² within city limits) plus Boulder. In US news, this becomes "a fire the size of Las Vegas" (367 km²) — the simile changes because the audience's reference points are in square miles. 500 km² ÷ 2.58999 = 193.1 sq mi. Rounded to 193. For the reverse conversion, use square miles to square kilometers.

Indonesia (1,904,569 km²) = 735,358 sq mi

The world's largest archipelagic state spans more than 17,000 islands. At 735,358 sq mi, Indonesia's land area sits between Mexico (761,600 sq mi) and Libya (679,400 sq mi) in the country-size rankings — a comparison that would be invisible if both numbers stayed in different units. Indonesia's maritime area adds another 3.2 million km² of territorial waters, which is not land and therefore shouldn't be converted, but sometimes is anyway in news graphics that confuse exclusive economic zones with dry ground.

Lake Superior (82,100 km²) = 31,700 sq mi

The largest freshwater lake by surface area on Earth at 82,100 km² (31,700 sq mi) — larger than South Carolina (32,020 sq mi) and roughly the size of Austria (32,383 sq mi). Lake Victoria, by comparison, covers 59,947 km² (23,146 sq mi), which is about 73% of Lake Superior's surface area. The Great Lakes collectively span 244,106 km² (94,250 sq mi), an area larger than the United Kingdom. These comparisons are a staple of geography textbooks, and every one of them was originally calculated in km² from shoreline shapefiles digitized in WGS84 coordinates.

The 2023 Survey Foot Sunset and What It Means for Area

On January 1, 2023, the National Institute of Standards and Technology officially deprecated the US survey foot. After 64 years of dual foot definitions — the international foot (exactly 0.3048 m, adopted in 1959) and the US survey foot (1200/3937 m, approximately 0.3048006 m) — NIST ended the ambiguity. The change, announced in 2019 and finalized in 2023, means that all new US surveys, engineering drawings, and legal descriptions should use the international foot. The two feet differed by 2 parts per million, which sounds trivial until you multiply it over 30 US states that were originally surveyed using the survey foot.

For area conversion, the sunset resolves a persistent source of confusion. Under the survey-foot definition, 1 survey square mile = 2.58999847 km² — a difference of 0.00001036 km² per square mile (about 10 m², or the size of a small bedroom) compared to the international definition. Over a township (36 sq mi), the accumulated difference reaches roughly 373 m². Over a state like Colorado, originally surveyed under the old foot, the discrepancy runs to dozens of square kilometers. NIST's deprecation means that going forward, there is exactly one conversion factor: 1 km² = 0.3861021585 sq mi, based on the international foot. Period. The old survey-foot conversion can be left in the 19th century where it belongs.

The practical effect on the ground is that GIS software — ArcGIS, QGIS, PostGIS — now defaults to international-foot-based projections. The EPSG registry, which catalogs thousands of coordinate reference systems, has gradually been phasing out survey-foot-based CRS definitions. If you are converting a shapefile that was digitized from a pre-2023 survey, check the metadata for the foot definition. If it says "US survey foot," your area values will be off by 2 ppm until you reproject. Most people will never notice. Surveyors will, and they have been dealing with this problem since 1959.

Engineering Context

The km²-to-sq-mi conversion is the first step whenever a global geospatial dataset lands on the desk of a US-based analyst. Satellite-derived land-cover classifications (MODIS, Landsat, Sentinel-2) all output pixel areas in metric units — typically square meters or hectares, aggregated to square kilometers. Converting to square miles is not a matter of preference; it is a requirement when the end user is a state planning agency, a county assessor, or a congressional district office that operates entirely in imperial units. The typical workflow: download a GeoTIFF or shapefile, run zonal statistics in QGIS or Google Earth Engine to get area totals in km², export to a spreadsheet, and apply the 0.386102 factor column-wide. The conversion is a spreadsheet formula that gets copied down 10,000 rows and forgotten about — until someone notices the Delaware watershed seems to have gained 3% of its area and traces the error back to a missing decimal place.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert km² to square miles?

Divide the km² value by 2.589988110336, or multiply by 0.3861021585. The shortcut km² × 0.3861 is accurate to four decimal places and works for anything that does not involve property boundaries or cross-border treaties. Example: 100 km² ÷ 2.58999 = 38.61 sq mi. The factor is exact because both units are defined from the same meter — the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement set 1 inch = 25.4 mm, defining the foot and mile exactly in metric terms. For the reverse operation, see the companion square miles to square kilometers converter.

What's bigger, a square kilometer or a square mile?

A square mile is larger by a factor of 2.59. One square mile equals 2.58999 km² — you can fit about 2.6 square kilometers inside a single square mile. Visualize it this way: walk 1 km straight, turn 90 degrees, walk another km — you have traced a square kilometer. To trace a square mile, walk 1.609 km (1 mile) on each side instead. The square mile edge is 61% longer, and the enclosed area is 159% larger. This is why country areas shrink by roughly 60% when converted from km² to sq mi — the unit grew, so the count fell.

How many km² is Manhattan?

Manhattan Island covers 59.1 km² of land area (22.8 sq mi). If you include the surrounding water within the borough's boundaries, the total is 87.0 km² (33.6 sq mi). Manhattan is 21.6 km long and 3.7 km wide at its widest, tapering to about 0.8 km at its narrowest near the Battery. Central Park occupies 3.41 km² (1.32 sq mi) — roughly 5.8% of the island's land area. The population density is approximately 27,000 people per km² (70,000 per sq mi), making it the densest county in the United States. For a sense of scale: if Manhattan had the population density of Wyoming (2.3 people per km²), only about 135 people would live on the entire island.

Why do global datasets use square kilometers instead of square miles?

Because 96% of the world's population and 100% of the world's scientific community use the metric system. Satellite sensors measure ground-sample distance in meters. Climate models grid the atmosphere in kilometers. Land-cover classifications define minimum mapping units in hectares (10,000 m²). The entire pipeline — sensor to pixel to classified area — is metric. Converting the output to square miles at the very end is a presentation step for an audience that thinks in miles. It is not wrong, but it adds a layer of translation that introduces rounding error. The square mile is not a unit that any satellite, climate model, or census algorithm produces natively. It is a unit that humans request after the computation is done.
