Cubic Feet to Cubic Meters — the 0.0283168 on every Bill of Lading

A 40-foot shipping container holds about 2,390 cubic feet — which the rest of the world writes as 67.7 cubic meters. That single multiplication, ×0.0283168, is the line every freight forwarder converts by hand before a container leaves port. It looks like an arbitrary decimal only until you see where it comes from: it is 0.3048 cubed, the foot defined in meters and then taken to the third power.

0.0283168 — the number on every Bill of Lading

International freight is billed in CBM (cubic meters) almost everywhere, but US domestic moves and many legacy quotes still list cargo in CFT (cubic feet). The translation between them is one constant: 1 ft³ = 0.028316846592 m³. A Bill of Lading that reads 67.7 CBM for a 40-foot container is really stating 2,390 cubic feet in SI clothing. Miss the conversion and a consolidator either under-bills a full container or rejects a shipment that "doesn't fit" the booked volume.

Why cubing the foot makes a small number

The factor is not arbitrary. One foot is exactly 0.3048 meters, so one cubic foot is (0.3048 m)³ — and 0.3048 × 0.3048 × 0.3048 = 0.028316846592. Two consequences follow. First, the number is exact, not measured, so there is no uncertainty in the constant itself. Second, cubing amplifies any shortcut: truncating to 0.0283 instead of the full value ships a 0.06% error. That is nothing for a ventilation calculation but everything when natural gas is custody-transferred in the millions of cubic feet and the bill is computed from the converted volume.

The container that is 67.7 m³ (and 2,390 ft³)

The standard 40-foot dry container has an internal volume around 2,390 ft³. Run it through ×0.0283168 and you get ~67.7 m³ — the figure ocean carriers use for rate tiers and stowage plans. A 20-foot container is roughly half that, about 33.2 m³ (1,172 ft³). The same arithmetic sizes a room: 1,000 ft³ is 28.32 m³, the volume of a small storage unit; 100 ft³ is 2.83 m³, about the inside of a large refrigerator. The cubic meter is simply the cubic foot rescaled into the unit the world's ports agree on.

Dimensional weight: when air freight bills the bigger number

Air cargo turns this volume into money through dimensional (volumetric) weight. A box may weigh 10 kg but occupy a volume whose cubic-meter figure, multiplied by a carrier factor, "weighs" 18 kg — and the airline bills the larger of the two. So the cubic-feet-to-cubic-meters step is not just descriptive; it directly sets the price. Get it wrong by a decimal place and a lightweight shipment is quoted as heavy, or a dense shipment sneaks under the threshold and the carrier loses revenue. Either way, the 0.0283168 constant is doing real financial work.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert cubic feet to cubic meters?

Multiply the number of cubic feet by 0.0283168. The exact factor is 0.028316846592, which is 0.3048 cubed — because one foot is exactly 0.3048 m, so one cubic foot is (0.3048 m)³. So cu m = cu ft × 0.028316846592.

Where is cubic-feet-to-cubic-meters used daily?

International shipping above all: freight is billed in CBM (cubic meters) worldwide but quoted in CFT (cubic feet) for US domestic moves. A 40-ft container is about 2,390 ft³ or 67.7 m³. HVAC airflow (CFM vs m³/s) and natural-gas metering use the same step.

How many cubic meters is 100 cubic feet?

100 cu ft × 0.0283168 = 2.83168 cu m. Air freight uses this volume for dimensional (volumetric) weight, billing the larger of the actual weight and the volume converted at a fixed factor — so the cubic-meter figure often decides the price.
