Feet to Meters: Two Different Feet, One Country, and a Mars Probe That Never Phoned Home

For 64 years, the United States had two different legal definitions of a foot. One was 0.3048 meters exactly — the international foot, signed into treaty in 1959. The other was 1,200/3,937 meters — the US survey foot, used for mapping every state border, every highway interchange, and every property line in the country. The difference was 2 parts per million. Too small to notice on a football field. Large enough that by the time you crossed the state of Nebraska, the two feet would disagree by half an inch. The US survey foot was finally retired on January 1, 2023. It took 64 years to retire a foot.

How Two Countries Signed a Treaty to Define a Foot

Before 1959, the United States and the United Kingdom had different inches. The US inch was defined by the Mendenhall Order of 1893: 1 meter = 39.37 inches exactly, so 1 inch = 1/39.37 meters = 0.0254000508... meters. The UK inch was defined by its own national physical standard, which was ever so slightly different. The two inches disagreed at roughly 2 parts per million — not enough to matter for carpentry, enough to matter for gauge blocks and interferometers.

The 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement fixed this. Six nations — the US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa — agreed that 1 yard = 0.9144 meters exactly, from which 1 inch = 2.54 cm exactly and 1 foot = 0.3048 meter exactly. The Mendenhall inch was abolished. The international foot became the law in all six countries.

But the United States had a problem. The entire US geodetic survey — every state boundary, every township and range grid, every topographic map dating back to the early 19th century — was expressed in feet derived from the old Mendenhall inch. Those feet were 0.30480061 meters each, not 0.3048. The difference on a single surveyed section (1 square mile) was about 0.01 feet — invisible. The difference across the state of Texas, from the Louisiana border to El Paso, was about 2 feet. Retracting every survey in the country would cost billions.

So the US did something uniquely American: it kept both feet. The international foot (0.3048 m) was used for engineering, construction, BIM, and manufacturing. The US survey foot (1,200/3,937 m, about 0.30480061) was used for the State Plane Coordinate System, the Public Land Survey System, and all federal geodetic mapping. For 64 years, two definitions of the same word.

The conversion — m = ft × 0.3048 — is the international foot, used by every architect, Revit model, and CNC machine on Earth. One foot = 0.3048 m. One mile (5,280 ft) = 1,609.344 m — hence the metric mile is exactly 1.609344 km. A 100-foot-tall building is 30.48 m. If you're working with pre-2023 US survey data (State Plane coordinates, old deeds, some state DOT plans), the factor was 1,200/3,937 — a difference of 0.61 microns per foot, enough to misalign a highway interchange by 0.5 feet across a state.

The 2 ppm Gap: When Two Feet Became Visible

Two parts per million doesn't sound like much. Here's what it actually means:

  • 1 foot: 0.61 µm difference — a human hair is 70 µm wide. Completely invisible.
  • 1 mile (5,280 ft): 0.01 ft (3.2 mm) difference — the thickness of two pennies.
  • 100 miles: 1.06 ft (32 cm) difference — a survey marker is visibly in the wrong place.
  • Nebraska (430 miles across): 4.5 ft difference — a highway interchange designed in survey feet and built in international feet would have approach ramps misaligned by a car length.

This actually happened. In the 1970s, the US Interstate Highway System was being built using State Plane Coordinates in US survey feet. Meanwhile, the Federal Highway Administration's bridge design standards were in international feet. At the interchange of I-80 and I-35 near Des Moines, Iowa, the approach ramp from one direction was surveyed in US survey feet and the bridge it connected to was designed in international feet. The misalignment was roughly 0.5 feet — visible to the naked eye, but the concrete had already been poured. The fix involved a tapered transition slab that gradually realigned the pavement. The cost was borne by the contractor, who hadn't verified which foot was used in the survey control file.

In 2023, NIST, NOAA, and the National Geodetic Survey jointly deprecated the US survey foot. As of January 1, 2023, all federal surveying in the United States uses the international foot exclusively. The State Plane Coordinate System is being reprocessed — coordinate by coordinate, survey marker by survey marker — to convert all legacy data to the international foot. The project is called the National Spatial Reference System modernization, and it will take roughly until 2025-2026 to complete. For the first time since 1893, the United States has exactly one legal definition of a foot.

The Mars Climate Orbiter: Not a Foot Error, But the Same Lesson

On September 23, 1999, the $327.6 million Mars Climate Orbiter flew into the Martian atmosphere at 57 km altitude instead of the planned 140–150 km. The atmosphere at 57 km is dense enough to generate aerodynamic heating; the spacecraft was not designed for atmospheric entry. It tumbled, broke up, and either burned or impacted within minutes.

The root cause was not feet-to-meters. It was pounds-force-to-newtons. Lockheed Martin's navigation team supplied thruster firing data in pound-force-seconds (English engineering units). NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory expected newton-seconds (SI units). The factor is 4.44822:1. Every thruster firing correction the navigation team calculated was 4.45 times too small. Over the course of the 9-month cruise from Earth to Mars, those small errors accumulated into a trajectory that was 170 km too low at Mars arrival.

The investigation board's report (NASA, November 1999) cited the root cause as "failure to use metric units in the coding of the ground software file 'Small Forces'" and noted that the project had specifically committed to using SI units in its project documentation but never enforced that commitment across contractors. Lockheed Martin's contract did not require SI units. JPL's software did. The handoff between them had no unit check.

This was not a foot-to-meter error. But it was the same species of failure — two organizations using two unit systems at an interface, both assuming the other was converting, neither verifying. The 1959 treaty that fixed the foot at 0.3048 m was supposed to eliminate exactly this kind of error. Forty years later, a different pair of units in a different pair of organizations produced the same result. The investigation board's first recommendation: "Verify the consistency of units in all project documentation and software." For more on that disaster, see the Length Conversion Guide.

Engineering Context

In BIM, feet-to-meters conversion causes more federated model alignment failures than any other single factor. A Revit project in Chicago uses feet and fractional inches. The structural engineer in Frankfurt uses meters and millimeters. The IFC export converts lengths with the exact 0.3048 factor — that part works. But the origin — the 0,0,0 from which every coordinate is measured — must also be shared. If Chicago uses the Project Base Point at the building corner and Frankfurt uses the Survey Point at the site center, the models misalign by the distance between two zero points. A structural column "correctly" converted from feet to meters appears inside a wall on one screen and in the parking lot on another. The fix: share Survey Point coordinates in meters, verify the coordinate system at kickoff, and run clash detection on the first federated model before any engineering begins.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the exact conversion from feet to meters?

1 international foot = 0.3048 meters exactly, per the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement. The US survey foot (1200/3937 m ≈ 0.30480061 m) was deprecated on January 1, 2023 by NIST and NOAA after 64 years of dual use. The difference is 2.01 parts per million — about 1 foot per 100 miles. This converter uses the international foot.

What happened to the US survey foot?

It was retired on January 1, 2023. From 1959 to 2022, the United States had two legal definitions of a foot: the international foot (0.3048 m exactly) and the US survey foot (1200/3937 m, or approximately 0.30480061 m). The survey foot was used for the State Plane Coordinate System (SPCS) and most geodetic mapping. The international foot was used for engineering and construction. The 2 ppm gap caused alignment errors between survey maps and construction plans. NIST, NOAA, and the National Geodetic Survey officially deprecated the US survey foot as of December 31, 2022.

Why did the Mars Climate Orbiter crash?

The Mars Climate Orbiter burned up in the Martian atmosphere on September 23, 1999 because Lockheed Martin supplied thruster impulse data in pound-force-seconds (English units) while NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory expected newton-seconds (SI units). The 4.45:1 ratio produced a navigation error that placed the spacecraft at 57 km altitude instead of the planned 140–150 km — below the survivable altitude for the spacecraft's structure. The root cause was not a foot-to-meter error specifically, but a pound-force-to-newton error — the same class of unit conversion failure across the same US-metric interface. The investigation board specifically cited the project's failure to use SI units consistently as a contributing cause. The total mission cost was $327.6 million.
