304.8 — the number every steel detailer knows by heart

In 1959, six nations — the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa — signed the International Yard and Pound Agreement. It was a quiet diplomatic document, not a treaty, not a law in every signatory. But it redefined the yard as exactly 0.9144 meters. Since a foot is one-third of a yard, the foot became exactly 0.3048 meters. Multiply by 1,000 and you get 304.8 millimeters — no approximation, no rounding, no wiggle room. The agreement's real consequence was physical: every structural steel connection designed in imperial units now had an exact metric equivalent. The number 304.8 was carved into the global engineering profession. Steel detailers have been multiplying by it ever since.

Walk into a steel fabrication shop in Shanghai or Chennai. The CNC plasma cutter reads millimeters. The operator loads a DSTV file — Deutsche Stahlbau-Verband, the German standard for steel data transfer — and every cut, every cope, every bolt hole is specified in whole and decimal millimeters. But the design drawing came from a structural engineer in Chicago, and it says "W24x76 beam, 40'-0" long, 3-inch bolt spacing." Somebody already ran the conversions. The beam length: 40 x 304.8 = 12,192 mm. The bolt spacing: 3 x 25.4 = 76.2 mm. The conversion is exact. But the numbers feel wrong to metric-trained eyes. Twelve thousand one hundred ninety-two. Seventy-six point two. A metric engineer sees 12,192 and thinks: why not 12,200? A 12,200 mm beam would be 40.026 feet. Close. But close does not close. The splice plates won't fit, the bolt holes won't align, and the ironworker reaches for the cutting torch.

The real danger is cumulative error across a row of connections. A 3-inch bolt pitch converts to 76.2 mm exactly. Twenty bolt holes along a 40-foot beam: 20 x 76.2 mm = 1,524 mm of bolt pattern. If the fabricator rounds each hole position to the nearest millimeter — 76 mm instead of 76.2 — the twentieth hole is off by 4 mm. A 4 mm misalignment on a splice plate means the bolt doesn't go through. The ironworker on site grabs the cutting torch. The inspector writes a non-conformance report. The engineer gets a phone call at 6 AM. All because someone rounded 76.2 to 76. The rule in steel shops that do international work: never round. Let the CNC controller handle the decimal places. Your job is to keep the number intact from the contract drawing to the DSTV file.

The 2006 Big Dig ceiling collapse in Boston killed one person and injured another. The National Transportation Safety Board investigation found that epoxy anchor specifications — originally written in metric, converted to imperial for procurement — contributed to confusion about load ratings and embedment depths. It was not a simple unit-conversion error. It was the accumulation of assumptions layered on top of conversions: metric design to imperial specification to field verification back to metric analysis. The steel industry absorbed the lesson: convert once, at the source, and carry every decimal place from that point forward. Rounding is a quality-control decision, not an arithmetic shortcut.

Today, 304.8 is baked into every steel detailing package — Tekla, SDS/2, Advance Steel. The software handles the multiplication silently. But a good detailer still knows the numbers cold. 20 ft = 6,096 mm. 30 ft = 9,144 mm. 8 ft = 2,438.4 mm. A great detailer knows that 20 ft 3-1/2 in is 6,184.9 mm without reaching for a calculator. The 304.8 is muscle memory, the way a carpenter knows 16 inches on center by feel. It is one of the very few exact imperial-to-metric conversions in engineering. Treasure it. Most of the others are a lot messier.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert feet to millimeters?

Multiply the number of feet by 304.8. Formula: mm = feet x 304.8. This is exact because 1 foot = 0.3048 meters = 304.8 millimeters by international definition.

Why convert feet to millimeters instead of meters?

Millimeters are the standard unit for precision manufacturing and technical drawings (ISO 2768). A US-designed part dimensioned as 2.5 feet converts to 762 mm — using millimeters keeps the number readable without decimal points and aligns with global manufacturing tolerances.

Is the feet-to-mm conversion exact?

Yes. Since 1959, 1 foot = 0.3048 meters exactly, and 1 meter = 1,000 millimeters exactly, so 1 foot = 304.8 millimeters exactly. There is no approximation in this conversion.

Engineering Context

Feet-to-millimeters bridges US structural design and global fabrication. A W24 beam drawn at 30 ft in a US office arrives at a metric shop as 9,144 mm. The 304.8 factor is exact — but it produces non-round numbers that look wrong to metric-trained eyes. 8 ft = 2,438.4 mm. That .4 at the end is not rounding error; it is what happens when an imperial dimension meets a metric shop drawing. Keep every decimal place. Your splice plates depend on it.
