The 100 m² Paris Apartment That's 1,076 sq ft in New York

Real estate is the largest purchase most people will ever make. And in a globalized market — where buyers in London browse listings in Barcelona, and investors in Singapore evaluate properties in Sydney — the square-meter to square-foot conversion is the first number they reach for. A 100 m² apartment sounds modest to a European buyer. The same space as 1,076 square feet sounds generous to an American. Same apartment, same layout, same price per unit of area — but the perception shifts because the number shifts. This is the psychology embedded in the conversion factor: 1 m² = 10.76391041671 sq ft. The seven significant figures matter for large commercial properties. For a studio apartment, rounding to 10.8 is close enough.

Why the Factor Isn't 10

sq ft = m² × 10.76391041671

Because 1 meter = 3.28083989501312 feet (from 1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly, 1959). Square both sides: 1 m² = 3.2808399² = 10.76391041671 sq ft. The squaring doubles the significant digits — and the rounding error. If you round the length conversion to 3.28 ft/m (0.03% error), the squared error becomes 0.06%. Over a 10,000 m² commercial property, that's 6 m² (65 sq ft) — enough to misprice a listing by the cost of a kitchen renovation.

Where the Conversion Lives Daily

Architects working on international projects live in both systems simultaneously. A firm in Tokyo designing a hotel for a client in Dubai will draw in millimeters (the Japanese standard) but present floor areas in square meters and square feet — because the investors and the furniture suppliers speak different measurement languages. The BIM (Building Information Modeling) software handles the conversion automatically, but the architect reviews both numbers because a rounding error in the conversion can propagate through the entire project. A single m² measurement error multiplied by 10.764, carried across 200 hotel rooms, adds up to 2,153 sq ft of incorrectly budgeted carpet — roughly $12,000 in material at mid-range hotel specifications.

Flooring retailers in the US price carpet by the square foot. European manufacturers ship tiles in square-meter boxes. A kitchen renovation requiring 25 m² of tile (269 sq ft) costs $2,690 at $10/sq ft — but if the contractor accidentally orders 25 sq ft (2.3 m²) because they misread the unit, the project stops mid-installation and the client waits three weeks for the restock. The conversion error is a single keystroke: mistaking m² for sq ft.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do European apartments seem smaller on paper than American ones?

They aren't — the numbers are just different. A 75 m² apartment in Berlin is 807 sq ft. A 1,200 sq ft apartment in Chicago is 111 m². The Berlin apartment isn't smaller; the number "75" is smaller than "1,200" because square meters are bigger units — each square meter packs 10.76 square feet. This perceptual gap creates a pricing illusion: European apartments appear cheaper per square foot to American buyers, even when the price per square meter is identical. Always convert before comparing. The math is simple: divide sq ft by 10.764 to get m², multiply m² by 10.764 to get sq ft.

How do real estate listing websites handle the sq m to sq ft conversion?

Most global listing platforms (Zillow, Rightmove, ImmobilienScout24) display area in the local primary unit with a secondary unit in smaller text — or offer a toggle. They convert using a fixed factor, usually 10.7639, and display one decimal place. This rounding means that a property listed as "1,076.4 sq ft" might show as "100.0 m²" when reversed, even though 1,076.4 ÷ 10.7639 = 99.998 m². The 0.002 m² discrepancy is legally negligible but technically imprecise. For legal documents (purchase agreements, mortgage applications), the primary number is always the one specified in the local deed registry, with the conversion provided for reference only.

What's the quickest mental conversion for property viewings?

Multiply or divide by 11. It's off by about 2% — a 100 m² apartment comes out to 1,100 sq ft instead of 1,076 — but close enough to compare properties while walking through them. For more precision, multiply by 10.8 (off by 0.3%). For exact numbers, use the calculator on this page. The "rule of 11" is the real estate agent's mental shortcut worldwide and has been for decades; it survives because the error is smaller than the typical discrepancy between a listing's advertised area and the actual measured area.
