Feet to Inches: Why 12? The Sumerians, the Romans, and the Number That Refused to Become 10

Twelve inches in a foot. Why twelve? Why not ten — like every other civilized unit system would insist? The answer starts roughly 5,000 years ago in Mesopotamia, with a counting method you can still do right now. Take your right hand. Look at your four fingers — index, middle, ring, pinky. Each has three bones (phalanges). Move your thumb across them: three bones per finger, four fingers, twelve positions. That's a Sumerian abacus, always with you, no batteries required. The Sumerians counted in base-12. Their numbering system — sexagesimal, base-60, built from 5 × 12 — gave us the 60-minute hour, the 360-degree circle, and the 12-inch foot. The metric system gave us base-10. The Sumerians already had a system. And in construction, where you need to divide lengths into halves, thirds, and quarters without fractions, base-12 is better than base-10. That's why the foot is still 12 inches. It's not stubbornness. It's arithmetic.

From Sumerian Bones to Roman Inches: A Short History of Twelve

The word "inch" comes from the Latin uncia — meaning one-twelfth. The Roman foot, or pes, was divided into 12 unciae. (The same root gives us "ounce" — originally one-twelfth of a Roman pound.) The Roman inch was about 24.6 mm — slightly shorter than the modern inch of 25.4 mm, because Roman feet were slightly smaller than modern feet. But the ratio was already fixed at 12:1, and it has never changed.

After the Roman Empire collapsed, the 12-inch foot survived through the medieval period, incorporated into the patchwork of local measurement systems across Europe. Each town had its own foot — the Paris foot, the Amsterdam foot, the London foot — all roughly the length of a human foot, all divided into 12 inches, but none of them exactly the same length. A bolt of cloth bought in Bruges and sold in Florence gained or lost inches in transit because the inches themselves were different sizes. This was not a minor inconvenience — it was a structural drag on trade that persisted for over a thousand years.

The 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement finally fixed the inch across the English-speaking world. Six nations — the US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa — agreed that 1 yard = 0.9144 meters exactly. From that: 1 foot = 0.3048 meters, and 1 inch = 25.4 millimeters exactly. The inch is now a metric unit — defined by the meter, which is defined by the speed of light. The Romans would not recognize the definition, but they would recognize the ratio: 12:1, same as it ever was.

Why 12 Beats 10 in the Real World

Ten divides evenly by 2 and 5. That's it. Twelve divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, and 6. If you're a carpenter building a cabinet and the plans call for a 12-inch shelf, you can divide it into halves (6"), thirds (4"), quarters (3"), or sixths (2") — all whole numbers. If the shelf were 10 inches, you can divide it into halves (5") and... that's it. Thirds give you 3.333... inches. Quarters give you 2.5 inches. Both are awkward to mark with a tape measure, and both introduce rounding errors that accumulate across a piece of furniture — or a house, or a bridge.

This is not nostalgia. This is the reason the imperial system's odd conversion factors — 12, 3, 1,760 — have survived despite two centuries of metric competition. A metric purist would say: just make the shelf 300 mm and divide by 2, 3, 4, or 6. And that works — in the metric system. But in the imperial system, the base-12 foot serves the same purpose: a highly divisible unit that makes mental arithmetic possible on a job site without a calculator. The 12-inch foot is base-12's last stand, and it's holding.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert feet to inches?

Multiply the number of feet by 12. inches = feet × 12. 1 ft = 12 in, 5 ft = 60 in, 6 ft = 72 in. The conversion is exact and has been since Roman times — the factor of 12 is one of the oldest continuously used measurement ratios in human history.

Why do US construction plans use feet and inches instead of decimal feet?

Because inches divide cleanly by 2, 3, 4, and 6 without producing fractions. A carpenter laying out studs 16 inches on center can find the midpoint (8"), the quarter point (4"), and the third point (5-1/3") without a calculator. If the same dimension were expressed in decimal feet (1.333 ft), the carpenter would need to convert to inches anyway before marking the wood. The foot-inch system keeps construction dimensions in whole numbers on the tape measure, which is the only display that matters on a job site. Some engineering disciplines (civil, structural) do use decimal feet — but those designs get converted to feet and inches for the people actually building them.

How is the inch defined today?

Since 1959, the inch has been defined as exactly 25.4 millimeters. This definition was established by the International Yard and Pound Agreement between the US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa. It means the inch is now legally a metric unit — defined via the millimeter, which is defined via the meter, which has been defined since 1983 as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second. The inch is no longer an independent unit. It's a fixed multiple of the meter: 1 in = 0.0254 m exactly. The Sumerian base-12 ratio (12 inches = 1 foot) survives, but the underlying length standard has been fully metricated.

Engineering Context

In US construction, the foot-inch system coexists with decimal feet in an uneasy truce. Structural engineers use decimal feet (a beam is 24.5 ft long). Architects use feet and fractional inches (a wall is 24'-6" long). The contractor reads the architectural drawings, which are dimensioned in feet and inches, and builds from the structural drawings, which may be in decimal feet, feet and inches, or — increasingly — both on the same sheet. The conversion between 24.5 ft and 24'-6" is trivial (multiply or divide the 0.5 by 12). The error comes when a dimension reads "24.5" and the reader doesn't know whether that's 24.5 feet or 24 feet 5 inches. The difference is 0.5 feet = 6 inches vs. 5 inches — a 1-inch discrepancy that, across a 200-foot building, can misalign every door frame on a floor. The standard practice: architectural drawings use dash notation (24'-6"); structural drawings use decimal feet (24.50 ft); survey drawings use decimal feet always. Never mix notations on a single drawing without a clear note in the title block. See the Length Conversion Guide for more on imperial measurement conventions.
