Hours to Minutes: The Babylonian ×60 That Runs Every Billable Hour, the 10,000-Hour Rule That Was Never Exactly 10,000, and Why Your One-Hour Meeting Just Cost $1,200

Multiply by 60. The ratio is older than the hour itself. The Babylonians chose base-60 around 2000 BCE because 60 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, and 30 —more divisors than any number under 100. That mathematical convenience became the minute —1/60 of an hour —and then the second —1/60 of a minute. Today, the same ×60 multiplication governs the billable hour in law firms (divided into 0.1-hour, 6-minute increments), the cost of a meeting ($20/minute with 8 people at $150/hr loaded cost), the 10,000-hour rule popularized by Malcolm Gladwell (10,000 h = 600,000 min), and every parking meter, conference room booking, and consulting engagement on Earth. The conversion is one multiplication. The history behind the number is 4,000 years of accountants, astronomers, and attorneys all agreeing that 60 was the right number to divide an hour into, even though nobody remembers why.

The Billable Hour: How ×60 Became a Business Model

The American Bar Association did not invent the billable hour. It was popularized in the 1950s and 1960s by large law firms as a way to standardize pricing across partners, associates, and practice areas. Before the billable hour, lawyers charged by the task —a flat fee for drafting a will, a contingent fee for a personal injury case. The billable hour shifted the risk from the lawyer to the client: the lawyer was guaranteed payment for every hour worked, regardless of outcome. The client paid for effort, not results.

The increment system —0.1 hour = 6 minutes —was adopted because it's the smallest fraction of an hour that maps to a psychologically defensible unit of work. Charge in 1-minute increments and the client receives a 52-page invoice detailing every minute of every day. Charge in 30-minute increments and a 2-minute email costs the same as a 30-minute phone call, and the lawyer's internal accounting loses granularity. The 6-minute increment splits the hour into 10 billable units, making the arithmetic simple: 0.1 hour × hourly rate = 1/10 of the rate. At $400/hour, 0.1 = $40. A 5-minute call = 0.1 = $40. A 9-minute email = 0.2 = $80. The system is transparent once you know the conversion. The client who doesn't know it receives a bill with "0.1" next to "Telephone conference re: deposition scheduling" and assumes 0.1 means one-tenth of something mysterious. It means 6 minutes. The arithmetic is: hours × 60 = minutes. The billing is: minutes ÷ 6 = tenths of an hour, rounded up. The difference between the two is the profit margin on every .1 entry on the invoice.

The 10,000-Hour Rule: How a Round Number Ate the Truth

In 1993, psychologist Anders Ericsson published a study of violin students at Berlin's Universität der Künste. He found that the most accomplished students —those judged by their professors as having the potential for international solo careers —had accumulated an average of 7,410 hours of deliberate practice by age 18. The next tier, judged as "good" but not exceptional, averaged 5,301 hours. The least accomplished averaged 3,420 hours. Ericsson titled the paper "The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance." The finding was that structured, focused practice —not talent, not casual playing —was the strongest predictor of performance.

Fifteen years later, Malcolm Gladwell published Outliers (2008), in which he wrote: "Ten thousand hours is the magic number of greatness." He cited Ericsson's study, Bill Gates's teenage programming hours, and the Beatles' Hamburg performances. The number 10,000 —600,000 minutes —became a pop-psychology factoid. Ericsson himself objected repeatedly, pointing out that his own data showed a wide range of practice hours among elite performers, that 10,000 was an average at age 20 —not a threshold —and that the quality of practice mattered more than the quantity. The damage was done. The hours-to-minutes conversion (10,000 × 60 = 600,000) is correct. The claim that 600,000 minutes of anything guarantees mastery is not. The difference between Ericsson's finding (deliberate practice predicts performance) and Gladwell's popularization (10,000 hours guarantees mastery) is a case study in how a round number —10,000 hours —can replace a nuanced finding in the public imagination. The minutes count. The threshold doesn't.

Worked Examples

0.1 h —6 min

The billable increment. A lawyer's unit of account. A consultant's email. A parking meter that takes a single coin. The shortest interval of human labor that anyone charges for in fractional-hour billing.

0.5 h —30 min

Half an hour. The standard meeting that should have been 15 minutes. The sitcom without ads. A treadmill session that feels like an hour. 0.5 × 60 = 30. The math is clean. The meeting is not.

1.5 h —90 min

A movie. A soccer match without stoppage time. A lecture. 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5. The decimal is cleaner than the fraction —nobody says "one and a half hours," they say "an hour and a half," and the payroll system converts it to 1.5 because payroll systems think in decimals, not English fractions.

8 h —480 min

The workday. 480 minutes. Subtract 30 minutes for lunch (unpaid in most states), 15 minutes of coffee/bathroom breaks, and 10 minutes of "how was your weekend" —roughly 425 minutes of actual work, or about 71% of the 480 minutes on the clock. The hours-to-minutes conversion is ×60. The minutes-to-productivity conversion is something HR departments have been trying to measure for a century.

Engineering Context

The hours-to-minutes conversion (×60) is the second multiplication in the Babylonian sexagesimal chain: days→hours (×24), hours→minutes (×60), minutes→seconds (×60). Each step multiplies by a different integer —none of which are powers of ten. The time system is the only major measurement system that resisted decimalization. The metric system decimalized length, mass, volume, and temperature. Time remained sexagesimal because the Babylonians got there first, the Greek astronomers standardized it, and every mechanical clock ever built was designed around the 12:60:60 division. Retooling every clock on Earth for decimal time would cost trillions and gain nothing. The sexagesimal system survives because the cost of replacing it exceeds the benefit. For the reverse direction, see minutes to hours (÷60). For the full chain: days to hours (×24), hours to seconds (×3,600), minutes to seconds (×60).

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do lawyers bill in 6-minute increments?

0.1 hour = 6 minutes is the smallest decimal fraction of an hour that maps to an integer number of minutes (0.1 × 60 = 6). A 1-minute increment would produce 0.01667-hour entries —decimal noise that no invoice formatter can display cleanly. A 10-minute increment (0.1667 hours) would lose granularity. Six minutes splits the hour into exactly 10 billable units, making the arithmetic transparent: multiply the number of 0.1-hour increments by the hourly rate divided by 10. At $300/hour, each 0.1 = $30. The 6-minute increment is not regulated by law —it's an industry convention adopted because it's the cleanest integer-multiple-of-60 that produces a workable billable unit. No bar association mandated it. Every law firm independently converged on it because the arithmetic was the least bad option among all possible ways to divide an hour into billable chunks.

How do I convert 8 hours and 45 minutes to decimal hours for a timesheet?

45 minutes ÷ 60 = 0.75 hours. 8 + 0.75 = 8.75 hours. Common conversions: 15 min = 0.25 h, 30 min = 0.50 h, 45 min = 0.75 h, 6 min = 0.10 h, 12 min = 0.20 h. The most frequent timesheet error is entering "8.45" —which the system interprets as 8.45 decimal hours (8 hours and 27 minutes), not 8 hours and 45 minutes. The 18-minute discrepancy per entry accumulates to roughly 1.5 hours of either overpayment or underpayment per biweekly period. The fix: convert minutes to decimal before entering. Divide by 60. The colon in "8:45" means hours and minutes. The decimal in "8.75" means decimal hours. Confusing the two formats costs either the employee or the employer real money, every pay period, silently, until someone notices the pattern.

Does practicing for 10,000 hours actually make you an expert?

No. Anders Ericsson's research showed that deliberate practice —focused, goal-directed, with immediate feedback and progressive difficulty —predicts expert performance. Total practice hours correlates with expertise but does not guarantee it. A person who casually plays guitar for 10,000 hours is not a master. A person who practices with a metronome, records themselves, analyzes their mistakes, and progressively increases difficulty for 5,000 hours may well be. The number of minutes is a proxy for the quality of those minutes. The proxy is useful. It is not the truth. If you have 10,000 hours to spend, spend them on deliberate practice, not on logging hours. The minutes ×60 conversion is correct. The science behind the number is more nuanced than the number itself.
