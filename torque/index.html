Torque Converters 10

Torque is the only common engineering quantity that shares its dimensional formula with energy — force × distance — yet means something completely different. A newton-meter of torque applied to a bolt is static. A joule of work moves something. Same units, different physics. That ambiguity, combined with three incompatible unit systems still in active use (US customary ft·lb/in·lb, SI N·m, and JIS/American pre-metrication kgf·m), makes torque conversion a genuine industrial hazard. Every converter below uses exact constants derived from the 1959 international yard and pound plus standard gravity (g₀ = 9.80665 m/s²).

Why Torque Conversion Matters

Torque is the rotational analogue of force — the twisting effort that turns a bolt, spins a crankshaft, or twists a driveshaft. It is measured as force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation. This means torque conversion involves two underlying conversions: the force unit and the distance unit. A foot-pound of torque is not one conversion — it is three: feet to meters, pounds to newtons, and the multiplication of both. The conversion factor 1 ft·lbf = 1.355818 N·m is not an arbitrary constant you look up in a table. It is the product of three treaty-defined numbers: 0.3048 × 0.45359237 × 9.80665.

The practical consequences of getting torque wrong are immediate and expensive. Over-torque a cylinder head bolt by a factor of 12 — confusing foot-pounds and inch-pounds on a torque wrench — and you strip the threads out of an engine block. Under-torque a structural bolt on a bridge bearing by the same factor and the joint relaxes under vibration. The most dangerous torque error is not the conspicuously wrong value. It's the value that feels right on the wrench but is off by a factor of 1.356 because someone confused N·m and ft·lb. A nut torqued to "100" in N·m is at 73.8 ft·lb. Torqued to "100" in ft·lb, it's at 135.6 N·m. Both numbers look like "100." The bolt doesn't care what you meant.

Modern torque wrenches often have dual scales — ft·lb on one side of the barrel, N·m on the other. But the graduations don't align. A click-type wrench set to "100 ft·lb" clicks at a different point than one set to "135.6 N·m." Split-beam wrenches, digital torque adapters, and hydraulic torque multipliers all read in different units depending on country of manufacture. A Japanese engine rebuild manual specifies cylinder head bolts at 12 kgf·m. A German manual for the same bolt pattern says 118 N·m. A US manual says 87 ft·lb. All three numbers are correct. All three are different. This is why torque conversion matters: because three mechanics in three countries can all do the job right and produce three different numbers on their wrenches — or one mechanic with the wrong conversion can destroy an engine at the last bolt in the torque sequence.

How Torque Units Relate

The foot-pound (ft·lbf) is the fundamental US customary unit of torque. It derives from the torque exerted by a one-pound force applied perpendicularly at the end of a one-foot lever arm. Because the pound and the foot are both defined exactly by the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement (1 ft = 0.3048 m; 1 lb = 0.45359237 kg), and standard gravity g₀ is defined by the 1901 CGPM at exactly 9.80665 m/s², every torque conversion involving these units is exact — with no measurement uncertainty — when expressed to enough decimal places.

The newton-meter (N·m) is the SI coherent derived unit of torque. One newton-meter is the torque produced by a one-newton force applied at one meter from the axis. The newton itself is derived from base SI units: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². Because the kilogram was redefined in 2019 via Planck's constant, every N·m conversion is now traceable to quantum electrodynamics rather than to a physical artifact in a Paris vault.

The kilogram-force-meter (kgf·m) is a gravitational metric unit that was widely used in continental Europe and Japan before SI adoption. It is the torque produced by a one-kilogram mass under standard gravity at one meter. In 1977, the CGPM officially deprecated the kilogram-force, but it persists in Japanese automotive specifications (JIS), in older German machine tool documentation, and in the industrial memory of a generation of engineers trained before SI became universal.

The inch-pound (in·lbf) is simply 1/12 of a foot-pound. It is used for low-torque applications: small fasteners, electronics assembly, instrument calibration, and bicycle components. A typical inch-pound torque wrench reads from 0 to 250 in·lb — equivalent to 0-20.8 ft·lb, a range that a foot-pound wrench cannot resolve accurately. Confusing the two — grabbing an inch-pound wrench when the spec calls for foot-pounds — produces a factor-of-12 error. That error has stripped threads in aircraft engine components, satellite fasteners, and surgical implant set screws.
