Square Miles to Square Kilometers: The 640-Acre Grid That Mapped a Continent

1 square mile = 2.58999 km². That number looks innocent until you realize it underlies every property deed in thirty American states, defines the shape of western agriculture, and is the reason your GPS shows a slightly different field boundary than the 1870 survey map hanging in the county courthouse. The Public Land Survey System turned the frontier into a grid of 1-square-mile sections, each exactly 2.589988110336 square kilometers, each exactly 640 acres, and each slightly misaligned with the planet it was drawn on.

The Grid That Won the West

In 1785, the Continental Congress faced a problem that had never been solved at scale: how to divide millions of square miles of unmapped territory into ownable, sellable, taxable parcels. The answer was the Public Land Survey System, devised primarily by Thomas Jefferson, Hugh Williamson, and a committee of surveyors who realized that irregular metes-and-bounds surveys — the kind used in the original colonies — would create endless boundary disputes as the nation expanded westward.

The solution was brutally geometric: a baseline running east-west, a principal meridian running north-south, and a grid of townships (six miles square) each containing thirty-six sections of one square mile. Every section was numbered identically. Every section was 640 acres. Every section could be described by a single string of coordinates: Township X North, Range Y West, Section Z. A child could learn it in an afternoon. A lawyer could settle a boundary dispute from an office in Philadelphia without ever seeing the land.

The system worked too well. By the time the last continental state was surveyed (Alaska, completed in the 20th century using a variant called the Alaska State Plane Coordinate System), the PLSS had produced approximately 2 billion acres of legally defined land — more than any other cadastral system in history. The 1-square-mile section became the atomic unit of American land ownership. And 1 square mile, squared into kilometers, is 2.589988110336 — a number that appears nowhere in the original survey manuals but that every modern GIS engineer needs daily.

The Conversion: From Chains to Meters

The math is straightforward because the underlying definitions were reconciled in 1959. Before 1959, the US used the US Survey foot (1200/3937 m ≈ 0.3048006 m) while the rest of the world used the International Foot (exactly 0.3048 m). After 1959, everyone agreed on 0.3048 m. One mile = 5280 feet = 1609.344 meters exactly. Squared: (1609.344)² = 2,589,988.110336 m² = 2.589988110336 km².

Worked Examples

1 section (1 sq mi) → 2.58999 km²

A standard PLSS section. 640 acres. About 250 city blocks (at 2.5 acres per block). Large enough for a mid-sized farm, a small airport, or the entire downtown core of a town like Leavenworth, Kansas.

640 acres → 2.58999 km²

The same number, expressed in acres. 1 section = 640 acres = 259 hectares (rounded). The acre-to-hectare conversion has its own complications: the US survey acre (43,560 ft² based on the old survey foot) differs from the international acre by 2 parts in 100,000. For a 100,000-acre cattle ranch, that's a 2-acre discrepancy — roughly three city lots.

Rhode Island (1,034 mi²) → 2,678 km²

The smallest US state. 1,034 × 2.58999 = 2,677.97 km². Official figure: 2,678 km². Close enough — the difference is rounding. Rhode Island is also the only US state whose official name includes "State of Rhode Island and Providence Plantations," which at 91 characters makes it the longest state name and arguably the only place name longer than the number of people who can correctly convert its area to metric on their first try.

Yellowstone (3,472 mi²) → 8,992 km²

The first national park. 3,472 × 2.58999 = 8,991.79 km². Official: 8,983 km². The 9 km² disagreement comes from how the boundary is measured (the park's irregular shape means different methods give different areas) and from the fact that Yellowstone sits partly in Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho, each of which may have surveyed portions differently.

Engineering Context

In GIS and cartography, the square-mile to square-kilometer conversion is the first step whenever data crosses from a legacy US government dataset (which uses statute miles) into a modern mapping platform (which defaults to meters/kilometers). The US Census Bureau publishes TIGER/Line shapefiles in decimal degrees and NAD83 projection; converting polygon areas requires either a database function like ST_Area(geography) (PostGIS) or manual reprojection. The naive approach — multiplying lat/lon degree counts by a constant factor — produces errors of 1–30% depending on latitude, because a degree of longitude shrinks toward the poles. At the equator, 1 degree of longitude ≈ 111.32 km. At 45°N, it's ≈ 78.85 km. A 1°×1° cell at the equator is ~14% larger than the same cell at 45°N. Your conversion factor changes with latitude. The constant 2.58999 is correct for planar geometry on a tangent plane. It is wrong for geographic coordinates on a sphere. Most web map tools don't tell you this. For the reverse direction, see square feet to square meters. For smaller land units: acres to square feet, hectares to acres, and acres to hectares.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 1 square mile exactly 2.59 square kilometers?

To five decimal places, yes: 2.58999. The 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement fixed 1 inch = 25.4 mm exactly, making 1 mile = 1609.344 m exactly. Squaring that gives 2,589,988.110336 m². Divide by 1,000,000 for km². The trailing decimals matter at scale: over 10,000 square miles, the difference between 2.59 and 2.58999 is about 1 km² — the size of Monaco.

What's the difference between a US survey acre and an international acre?

The US survey acre is based on the pre-1959 US survey foot (1200/3937 meter). 1 survey acre = 4046.87261 m². The international acre uses the post-1959 foot (exactly 0.3048 m). 1 international acre = 4046.85642 m². Difference: 16.2 cm² per acre — about the size of a credit card. Over 640 acres (one section), that's 10.4 m² — a 3.2×3.2 m patch. Not enough to build a house, but enough to lose in court if you're selling land by the acre.

Why does my GPS show a different area than my deed?

Because your deed was measured in 1887 with a chain and compass along physical markers (rocks, trees, iron posts). Some of those markers have moved, been lost, or were never precisely located. GPS measures in WGS84 coordinates on an ellipsoid model of Earth. The original survey was done on a local tangent plane assuming flat ground. The combination of measurement error, marker movement, datum shift (NAD27 to NAD83), and coordinate transformation can shift a boundary by several meters. Over a 640-acre section, a few meters of shift changes the computed area by fractions of an acre. Courts generally favor the original monument locations over calculated coordinates — the physical rock wins over the satellite. This principle is called "following the footsteps of the original surveyor," and it's why land surveying is still a licensed profession despite the existence of GPS.

Which countries still officially use square miles?

The United States, Liberia, Myanmar, and to varying extents the United Kingdom (for road distances and informal usage). Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, India, and nearly every former British colony switched to metric for land registration decades ago, though rural populations often still think in acres informally. The UK's Ordnance Survey switched entirely to metric in the 1990s, but estate agents continue to advertise houses in "square feet" because buyers refuse to learn what a square meter feels like. The US will likely keep square miles indefinitely — the cost of re-platting every county recorder's office exceeds the benefit of switching, and nobody wants to be the politician who told farmers their deeds are now in a foreign language.
