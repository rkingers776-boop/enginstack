Feet to Yards: A King's Arm, a Bolt of Cloth, and the Unit That American Football Refuses to Give Up

Three feet make a yard. Divide by three. The yard is an old unit — older than the meter, older than the British Empire, older than the Magna Carta. Medieval legend says King Henry I defined it as the distance from his nose to the tip of his thumb. The less glamorous truth: it was a cloth merchant's measuring stick. A "yard of cloth" was a standard bolt width. The word comes from the Old English gyrd — a straight branch or rod. For 900 years, the yard has been the English-speaking world's answer to the meter. It has survived metrication, industrialization, and two World Wars. And every Sunday from September to February, 22 men in helmets line up on a field measured entirely in yards.

The Cloth Trade Built the Yard. The Industrial Revolution Almost Killed It.

In medieval England, the most important manufactured good was woolen cloth. The yard was the standard unit of cloth length — a bolt of standard-width fabric measured in yards. Every market town had a yard stick, a physical wooden rod exactly one yard long, kept by the guild and used to settle disputes between buyers and sellers. The yard stick was the standard, and it was enforced by law. Selling short yardage was fraud. The penalties ranged from fines to the destruction of the cloth to public shaming in the town square.

The yard was formally standardized by the British Weights and Measures Act of 1824, which defined it as exactly 3 feet. By that point, the yard had already spread through the British Empire — to America, Canada, Australia, India, and a hundred other colonies. It became the default unit for anything between a foot (too small for land measurement) and a mile (too large for building). A room was measured in feet. A field was measured in yards. A road was measured in miles. The yard filled the gap between domestic and geographic scale.

The meter was supposed to replace it. During the French Revolution, the metric system's designers specifically targeted the yard as redundant — the meter (roughly 1.09 yards) was meant to serve the same human-scale function, but within a decimal system. The meter succeeded almost everywhere. The yard held on in Britain until the 1970s and in the United States indefinitely. It survives today in three domains: sports (football, golf, track and field in some countries), textiles (a "yard of fabric" is still the standard retail unit at American fabric stores), and construction materials (concrete is sold by the cubic yard; a standard concrete truck carries 10 cubic yards). The yard is obsolete in every technical sense. It is also deeply embedded in how Americans measure the physical world.

A Cubic Yard of Concrete: Where the Yard Still Means Money

A cubic yard (often just called "a yard" by contractors) is the standard unit for concrete, topsoil, gravel, and mulch in the United States. One cubic yard = 27 cubic feet (3 ft × 3 ft × 3 ft). A typical concrete truck carries 10 cubic yards — roughly 270 cubic feet of wet concrete. The price of concrete is quoted per cubic yard: as of 2026, roughly $150-$200 per cubic yard for standard 3,000 psi mix, delivered. When a contractor orders "20 yards of concrete" for a foundation pour, they mean 20 cubic yards — 540 cubic feet, about 40 metric tons of material. The yard is hiding inside one of the largest material supply chains in the world. The conversion to feet is everywhere: a foundation wall is 8 inches thick, 4 feet tall, and 100 feet long. That's (8/12) × 4 × 100 = 266.7 cubic feet ÷ 27 = 9.88 cubic yards of concrete. Order 10 yards. The math is awkward — dividing by 27 is nobody's favorite operation — but the supply chain is built on it, and it's not changing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert feet to yards?

Divide the number of feet by 3. yards = feet ÷ 3. 3 ft = 1 yd, 10 ft = 3.333 yd, 30 ft = 10 yd, 300 ft = 100 yd. The conversion is exact — the yard has been exactly 3 feet since at least the 14th century, and was formally codified in the 1824 British Weights and Measures Act.

Is the yard still used outside the United States?

Limited use. The UK officially adopted the metric system in the 1970s but yards survive on road signs (distances in miles and yards until the late 2010s, now largely metric) and in golf (yards to the pin). Canada uses meters for most official purposes but carpenters and golfers still think in yards. Australia and New Zealand are almost entirely metric. The yard's strongest remaining holdout is American sports — football, golf, and track and field (where some events are still reported in yards at the high school level). In international sport, the meter has completely replaced the yard.

Why do we say 'a yard of ale'?

A yard of ale is a traditional English drinking vessel — a glass tube roughly one yard long with a bulb at the end, holding about 2.5 imperial pints (1.4 liters). It originated in 17th-century English coaching inns and became a ceremonial challenge: drink the entire yard without stopping. The name refers to the length of the glass — one yard — not the volume. The yard-of-ale glass is one of the few surviving cultural artifacts that connects the medieval yard (a measuring stick) to the modern yard (a unit of length). It's also very difficult to drink from without spilling on yourself.

Engineering Context

The foot-to-yard conversion (÷3) is simple but creates a persistent fraction problem. One foot is 0.333... yards — a repeating decimal that cannot be represented exactly in a finite number of digits. In CAD and BIM software, this rounding error is typically handled by storing all lengths internally in a base unit (millimeters or feet) and converting to display units on the fly. But in manual quantity takeoffs — which still happen on construction sites every day — a contractor dividing feet by 3 to get yards for a concrete order can accumulate rounding errors. A foundation wall 100'-0" long is 33.333... yards. Multiply by a 4'-0" height and an 8" thickness (8/12 = 0.666... ft), and the cubic yardage is (33.333... × 4 × 0.666...) ÷ 27 — a chain of repeating decimals that almost guarantees a rounding discrepancy between the contractor's estimate and the ready-mix supplier's invoice. The fix is to do all quantity calculations in feet first, convert to cubic yards at the very end, and round up to the nearest quarter yard for ordering.
