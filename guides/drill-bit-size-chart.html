Drill Bit Size Chart: Decimal, Number, Letter and Metric Sizes in Inches and Millimeters

A number drill, a letter drill and a fractional drill can all cut the same hole, and a metric drill can cut the same hole again under a plain millimeter callout. This page gives all four series with their decimal inch and millimeter equivalents, states what the fractional sizes are exactly rather than approximately, and explains the one rounding decision that makes two published charts disagree about a size neither of them has got wrong.

1. Four Sizing Systems, One Hole

The first thing to know about drill sizes is that there is no single system, and never was. Four naming schemes are in daily use, each one inherited from a different trade and a different era, and each one covering only part of the range. A fractional drill is named by its diameter as an ordinary fraction of an inch. A number drill is named by an old wire-gauge number, where a larger number means a smaller drill. A letter drill uses the alphabet the same way, but running the other way, so a later letter is a larger drill. A metric drill is named by its diameter in millimeters and needs no conversion at all.

The systems interleave rather than nest, which is the part that trips people up. Number drills fill the small end of the range and run continuously into the letter series at the top; letter drills then stop at Z, which is still below one half inch; fractional drills cover the small sizes in coarse steps and take over properly above that. A hole of a given size can therefore be reached by a fractional drill, a number drill or a letter drill, and the three designations will not resemble each other.

That is why the working method is always the same: reduce the designation to its decimal diameter, and compare decimals. Everything else on this page follows from that one move.

One hole, several names

Take a hole about a quarter of an inch across. It can be cut by a 1/4 inch fractional drill, by a number drill or a letter drill of the same diameter, or by the nearest metric size, and all three are formally different designations for the same hole. The tables below put all four series on one decimal scale, which is the only scale on which they can be compared.

2. Fractional Sizes, 1/64 inch to 1 inch

Fractional drills are the sizes you find in a hardware store index and the ones a drawing usually calls for outside precision work. They step by a constant 1/64 of an inch, so the series is evenly spaced in name even though it is coarse by any modern standard: there are only two sizes between 3/16 inch and 1/4 inch.

The fractional series is also the only one of the four whose true value is exact. Three thirty-seconds of an inch is exactly 0.09375 inch, not an approximation of it, because it is a ratio of two whole numbers. Every other series on this page was defined by measuring or by convention, and carries a decimal that stops where the standard chose to stop. That distinction is what produces the disagreement discussed in the sixth section: exact values survive arithmetic, while rounded values do not.

3. Number Sizes, #1 to #80

Number drills descend from the old wire gauge, which counted the number of drawing passes a rod took to reach a diameter: more passes, thinner wire, larger number. The drill series kept the convention, so #1 is the largest at 0.2280 inch and #80 is the smallest at 0.0135 inch, and the inversion holds throughout the series: #60 is 0.0400 inch while #40, a smaller number, is 0.0980 inch. Reading a number drill index without noticing that the scale runs backwards is a classic and expensive error.

The purpose of the series is resolution. Where fractions step in gaps of 0.015625 inch, number drills close up to steps of a thousandth or two at the small end, and that is the resolution thread work needs. Number drills are therefore the natural home of the tap drill, and the series is dense exactly where threads are small.

4. Letter Sizes, A to Z

Letter drills continue the same job as number drills where the number series runs out about a quarter inch, and they fill the gap up to one half inch. Unlike the number series, the letter series runs the intuitive way: A is the smallest at 0.2340 inch and Z is the largest at 0.4130 inch, so a later letter is a bigger drill.

Twenty-six letters cover 0.1790 inch of range, which works out at an average step near 0.0072 inch, but the steps are not even. The series exists to provide tap drills for inch threads in the middle of the range, and it is spaced to suit those threads rather than to be uniform. Several letters are better known by the thread they drill than by their own diameter, which is the subject of the tap drill section below.

One entry in this table deserves a note, because it is the only place in the whole chart where a published copy disagrees with the standard. Letter K is 0.2810 inch in the standard tables and in most published copies, but at least one widely copied online table prints 0.2811 inch. The difference is a tenth of a thousandth of an inch, well under any drill tolerance, and it is almost certainly a transcription slip rather than a different standard. If you find 0.2811 in a chart you are using, that chart has a typo, not a different convention.

5. Metric Sizes, 1.0 mm to 25 mm

Metric drills are the simple case, because the designation is the diameter. There is nothing to invert and nothing to round: an 8 mm drill is 8 mm, and the millimeter figure on the drill shank is the measurement rather than a label for it. The series below follows the nominal sizes of ISO 235, stepping in tenths of a millimeter up to 10 mm and in half millimeters from 10.5 mm to 25 mm.

Because the metric size is exact by definition, a metric drill has no rounding ambiguity at all, and the only figure on this page derived from it is the decimal inch equivalent in the second column. That is the reverse of the situation in the fractional series, where the inch value is exact and the millimeter value is derived. It is a useful check on any conversion: the series whose designation is a whole quantity is the one whose other column carries the rounding.

6. Why Two Charts Disagree in the Third Decimal

Put two published drill charts side by side and some of the millimeter figures will not match, even though both charts are using the same standard and neither has made an arithmetic mistake. The cause is a single decision about rounding order, and it only affects the fractional series, because that is the only series whose true value has more digits than the value printed on the chart.

The exact value of 3/32 inch is 0.09375 inch. Multiply that by 25.4 and you get 2.381 mm. But a chart that prints the inch figure first must round it to four decimals, which gives 0.0938 inch, and converting that rounded figure gives 2.383 mm. Both readings are defensible; they simply round at different points in the chain.

This page converts the exact value, never the printed value. The rule matters because the printed inch figure is a lossy representation, and converting a rounded number compounds one rounding with another. There are 32 fractional sizes in the table above where the two orders give different millimeter figures. The table below shows the common ones.

The differences are all 0.001 or 0.002 mm, which is a fortieth of a typical drill tolerance and has no practical consequence. The reason to understand them is diagnostic: if a chart you are checking differs from this one by one or two thousandths of a millimeter on a fractional size, it has rounded in the other order and is fine. If it differs by more than that, or if it differs on a number or letter drill, it has an actual error, because those series have no rounding to argue about. Whether a substitution is acceptable depends entirely on the fit the hole is for. The errors above run from a few hundredths to a few tenths of a millimeter. 8. Why So Many Tap Drills Are a Number or a Letter

A tap drill has to leave the right amount of material for the thread: enough that the thread is strong, not so much that the tap has to cut the whole depth of the thread in one pass. The conventional choice leaves about 75 per cent of a full thread, which for a given pitch lands on a diameter that almost never coincides with a convenient fraction. That is precisely why the number and letter series exist, and why so many tap drills are listed as a number or a letter rather than as a fraction.

The table gives the standard 75 per cent tap drill for the coarse inch threads most often cut in a shop, drawn from the number and letter tables above so that the diameters agree with the rest of this page. A 1/4-20 thread takes a #7 drill, a 5/16-18 takes an F, and a 1/2-13 takes 27/64 inch: three different series for three threads of a similar size, which is the whole point.

Two practical notes. The 75 per cent figure is the one for aluminum, brass and plastics; steel, stainless and cast iron are often started with the 50 per cent drill, which is a size or two larger, because a coarser thread is easier to cut in a hard material and the loss of thread strength is acceptable. And a tap drill is a starting size, not a guarantee: the finished thread depth depends on the tap itself and on how far it is driven, so a hole that meets its drill diameter can still fail a thread gauge.

The same reasoning about material and clearance applies to sheet and plate, where the sizing chart problem is a different one: there the number is a legacy gauge rather than a wire number, and the mapping to thickness depends on the metal. The sheet metal gauge chart covers that case, including why one gauge number means a different thickness in every metal.

9. Sizing a Hole on a Drawing

The most useful habit with drill sizes is the same one that applies to every legacy unit: specify the hole by its diameter and its tolerance, not by the drill that happens to cut it. A decimal inch or a millimeter figure has one meaning and is the figure an inspector will measure; a drill designation is a convenient name for a cutter, and several different cutters can answer to nominally the same callout once tolerances are in play.

Three rules of thumb carry most of the benefit:

  • Give the hole diameter and its tolerance, and add the drill size in brackets only where the shop floor actually needs it. The bracket is a convenience, and the diameter is the requirement.
  • Do not bridge unit systems on the shop floor. If the drawing is in millimeters, order the metric drill; if it is in inches, use the inch index. A substituted drill is a silent error that no inspection step is looking for.
  • Remember that drill tolerance is a range, not a point. A nominal drill cuts slightly oversize in most materials, so a hole specified to a tight tolerance is reamed or bored after drilling rather than drilled to size.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a drill bit size chart?

It is a table that converts a drill designation into the hole it actually cuts, in inches and in millimeters. Four naming systems are in daily use and none of them covers a whole range: fractional sizes step in sixty-fourths of an inch, number sizes run from #1 down to #80, letter sizes run from A up to Z, and metric sizes are simply the diameter in millimeters. Because the four series interleave, one nominal hole can be called several different things, and the only way to compare them is to reduce every designation to its decimal diameter.

Why are drill bits sized in numbers and letters instead of fractions?

Because fractions leave gaps. A sixty-fourths ladder has two sizes between 3/16 inch and 1/4 inch, and neither lands where a thread needs its pilot hole. Number drills fill the small end of that range, from #1 at 0.2280 inch down to #80 at 0.0135 inch, stepping in small increments so that a tap drill can be matched to a thread. Letter drills then continue upward through the middle of the range, from A at 0.2340 inch to Z at 0.4130 inch. Between them, the two series give a size every few thousandths of an inch, which is what thread work needs and what fractions cannot give.

Why does one chart say 3/32 inch is 2.381 mm and another say 2.383 mm?

Because the two charts round at different points. Three thirty-seconds of an inch is exactly 0.09375 inch, and that exact value times 25.4 is 2.381 mm. Many printed charts round the inch figure to four decimals first, which gives 0.0938 inch, and then convert, which gives 2.383 mm. Both numbers are correct readings of the same drill. The difference is two thousandths of a millimeter, roughly a fortieth of a typical drill tolerance, and it appears only in the fractional series, because fractional sizes are the only ones whose true value has more digits than the printed one.

How do I convert a drill size to millimeters?

Reduce the designation to its decimal diameter and multiply by 25.4. A fraction becomes numerator divided by denominator: 3/32 is 0.09375 inch, so 2.381 mm. A number or letter drill is already a decimal, so #1 at 5.791 mm and #80 at 0.343 mm are read straight off the tables above. A metric drill needs no conversion at all, because the designation is the diameter. The tables on this page give both columns so the arithmetic is never done twice.

Is a 5/16 inch drill the same as an 8 mm drill?

No, and the gap is larger than it looks. Five sixteenths of an inch is 0.3125 inch, which is 7.938 mm, while an 8 mm drill is 8 mm by definition, which is 0.3150 inch. The two differ by 0.0625 mm, about 0.0025 inch. On a clearance hole for a bolt that is harmless. On a hole that will be tapped, on a press fit, or on a dowel location it is the difference between a joint that holds and one that does not. Always reduce both designations to decimals before deciding they are interchangeable.

Sources and Further Reading

  • ANSI/ASME B94.11M, Twist Drills. The standard that fixes the number series #1 to #80 and the letter series A to Z. The decimal diameters used on this page were taken from the standard tables and checked against three independent published copies; the three agree on all eighty number drills and on twenty-five of the twenty-six letter drills. The single disagreement is letter K, printed as 0.2810 inch by two sources and 0.2811 inch by one. Both this page and the majority of the standard tables use 0.2810 inch.
  • ISO 235, Parallel shank jobber and stub series drills. Defines the metric nominal diameters used in the metric table, which is a nominal series rather than a computed one: the millimeter figure is the size, and nothing is derived from it except the inch equivalent.
  • NIST Special Publication 811, Guide for the Use of the International System of Units (SI), for the exact factor relating inch and millimeter, 25.4 millimeters per inch, and the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement that fixed it.
  • Unified thread standards ANSI/ASME B1.1, for the UNC threads quoted in the tap drill table. Tap drill sizes are chosen to leave roughly 75 per cent of a full thread in aluminum, brass and plastics and 50 per cent in steel, stainless and cast iron; the figures in the table are the 75 per cent values that appear in the standard machinist handbooks.
