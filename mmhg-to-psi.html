mmHg to psi — your blood pressure, on the gauge scale

Put a blood pressure cuff's reading on an American pressure gauge and the numbers look wrong: 120/80 mmHg becomes 2.32/1.55 psi, tiny fractions that seem too small to matter. They are correct — mmHg and psi are just different rulers for the same pressure. The Number That Looks Wrong but Isn't

Every American learns "120 over 80" and never questions the unit. Convert it to psi and the reading becomes 2.32 over 1.55 — numbers that look like a tire that needs air, not a healthy heart. The reason the same pressure reads 120 in one unit and 2.32 in the other is geometry: psi spreads the force over a square inch of artery wall, while mmHg measures the height of a dense mercury column that concentrates the same pressure into a thin tube. Mercury is 13.6 times denser than water, and a psi's worth of force lifts a 51.7 mm column of it. The scale difference is a physical property, not a medical convention — which is exactly why the conversion factor is exact.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert mmHg to psi?

Multiply by 0.0193368: psi = mmHg × 0.019337. A blood pressure of 120 mmHg is 120 × 0.019337 = 2.32 psi. The factor is exact — the reciprocal of 51.7149252, the ratio of the defined psi and mmHg.

What is normal blood pressure in psi?

The healthy reading of 120/80 mmHg is 2.32/1.55 psi. It looks startlingly small because psi spreads force over a full square inch while the mercury column concentrates it into a thin tube — but it is the same pressure, and it is why mmHg exists: to give physiological pressures convenient two-digit numbers.

Why does medicine use mmHg instead of psi?

Because the first blood pressure cuff, Riva-Rocci's 1896 sphygmomanometer, read directly off a mercury column — millimeters of mercury was the instrument's own scale, and clinical practice never left it. A mercury column at 0 °C is defined as 133.322387415 Pa, so the conversion to psi (6,894.757 Pa) is exact: 120 mmHg = 2.32 psi.
