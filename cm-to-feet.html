How to Convert CM to Feet

Here's the thing nobody tells you about converting centimeters to feet: the division takes half a second, but the formatting takes all the actual work. 180 cm ÷ 30.48 = 5.9055 feet. Correct — and completely useless in conversation. Nobody says "I'm 5.9055 feet tall." You have to isolate the integer feet (5), take the fractional remainder (0.9055), multiply by 12 to get inches (10.866), and then decide what to do with 10.866 inches. Round down to 5'10"? Round up to 5'11"? Display as 5'10.9"? Every app, every website, every piece of medical software that converts centimeters to feet has to make this same decision, and they don't all make the same one.

The most visible victim of the cm-to-feet formatting problem might be furniture assembly. IKEA's PAX wardrobe system — probably the most-assembled piece of furniture on Earth — is designed in millimeters and published in centimeters everywhere except the United States. A 200 cm PAX frame shows up on the US website as 78 3/4" wide, which is 6 feet 6 3/4 inches. Three different units in a single dimension. The 201 cm variant becomes 79 1/8", or 6 feet 7 1/8 inches. Each conversion step introduces a rounding decision: centimeters to inches (divide by 2.54, round to nearest 1/16"), inches to feet-inches (divide by 12, round fractional remainder to nearest 1/8").

This isn't academic. I've seen a contractor build a closet insert for a "200 cm PAX" only to discover the unit that arrived was the 201 cm version — the customer had misread the metric label on the box and ordered by the "200 cm" category name instead of the actual SKU dimension. A 1 cm difference at wardrobe scale produces a 3/8" gap at the wall. That gap gets filled with trim, but the trim was supposed to sit flush. The problem compounds because IKEA's own internal specifications use millimeter precision: that 200 cm wardrobe is actually 2,000 mm, and the 201 cm is 2,010 mm. The centimeter rounding happens at the catalog level, before the conversion to imperial even begins.

While commercial aviation runs on feet for altitude worldwide — FL350 means 35,000 feet regardless of what country you're flying over — the conversion hazard runs in the opposite direction. Some European aeronautical charts publish obstacle heights in centimeters on supplemental data pages. A radio tower listed as "15240 cm" is 500 feet tall, a threat to aircraft. But scan that number quickly in a monospaced font without digit grouping, and your eye sees "15240" as a number that could begin with "1524.0" — which, mentally shifted one decimal place, reads as 1,524 cm, or 50 feet. The visual similarity between 500 feet and 50 feet in their centimeter representations has been flagged in at least two ICAO safety reports. The fix is digit grouping: 15,240, not 15240. Not every chart producer separates thousands.

If you've ever used a dating app with a height filter, you've encountered the cm-to-feet rounding war without knowing it. A European user listing 183 cm gets shown to Americans who set their filter to "6 feet and up." But 183 cm = 6.0039 feet = 6 feet 0.05 inches — barely over the line. Some apps use floor rounding and display 6'0". Some truncate and show 5'11". Some compute to the nearest inch, and since 183 cm is closer to 6'0" (183.0 cm vs. 182.88 cm for a true 6 feet), they round up. The same person can appear as 6'0" on Hinge, 5'11" on Bumble, and 183 cm on Tinder's metric display. There's no standard. Every platform picks its own rounding convention, and a tenth of an inch determines whether you clear the filter.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert cm to feet?

Multiply cm by 0.0328084. Formula: ft = cm × 0.0328084. For a quick estimate, divide cm by 30 (e.g., 180 cm ÷ 30 ≈ 6 ft).

How do I convert cm to feet and inches?

First get decimal feet (cm × 0.0328084), then multiply the decimal part by 12 to get inches. Example: 180 cm = 5.9055 ft → 0.9055 × 12 = 10.87 in — approximately 5'11".

Is 180 cm considered tall?

180 cm (5'11") is above average male height in the US (175.3 cm / 5'9") and UK (175.3 cm), but below average in the Netherlands (183.8 cm / 6'0"). Context matters — use this converter to compare against your local reference.

Engineering Context

The cm-to-feet conversion is deceptively simple on paper and persistently annoying in practice. The raw arithmetic — divide by 30.48 — is a one-liner. But the result is a decimal number that nobody actually uses in conversation. Converting 175 cm to 5.74 feet is correct and useless. The real engineering challenge is the formatting layer: extracting the integer feet from the decimal result, converting the fractional remainder to inches, and then deciding the rounding precision. Every application that displays heights to American users — fitness trackers, patient portals, dating apps — has to solve this problem independently, and they all solve it slightly differently.
