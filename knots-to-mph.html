Knots to MPH — 1.15078, the Nautical-to-Statute Bridge

How to Convert Knots to Miles Per Hour

A knot is a nautical mile per hour. A nautical mile is 1,852 metres exactly (since the 1929 International Hydrographic Conference). A statute mile — the mile on your car's odometer — is 1,609.344 metres exactly (since the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement). Divide the two: 1,852 ÷ 1,609.344 = 1.15078. That's your conversion factor. A knot is always, permanently, 15.078% faster than a mile per hour. The gap is large enough to matter — a 20-knot harbour entrance that you read as 20 mph puts you on the rocks at 23 mph equivalent — and it has mattered, repeatedly, in real navigation accidents.

The knot didn't always equal exactly 1.15078 mph. Before 1954, the United States used its own nautical mile definition of 6,080.2 feet (about 1,853.25 metres), which produced a slightly different knot-to-mph ratio. The US adopted the international nautical mile of 1,852 metres in 1954, bringing American charts into alignment with the rest of the world. If you're navigating with a pre-1954 US chart — unlikely today, but they still surface in antique shops and maritime museums — your knots are about 0.07% off from modern knots.

The Conversion Formula

mph = knots × 1.15078

Worked Examples

Example 1: Convert 20 knots to mph

20 × 1.15078 = 23.02 mph (a container ship at cruise — slower than a car on a suburban arterial, but it's moving 200,000 tonnes of cargo and burning 250 tonnes of fuel per day to do it)

Example 2: Convert 50 knots to mph

50 × 1.15078 = 57.54 mph (an America's Cup AC75 foiling monohull in 15 knots of true wind — the boat is moving nearly four times faster than the wind driving it, which is physically impossible for a displacement hull and only works because the foils lift the entire 6.5-tonne vessel out of the water)

Example 3: Convert 100 knots to mph

100 × 1.15078 = 115.08 mph (a Category 3 hurricane's sustained wind speed — structural failure begins at this threshold. A 100-knot typhoon is doing highway speeds, and the wind load on a vertical surface at this velocity is about 240 kg per square metre, enough to peel the roof off a framed house)

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is a knot faster than a mile per hour?

Because a nautical mile is longer than a statute mile. A nautical mile is one arcminute of latitude — roughly 1,852 metres. A statute mile is 1,609 metres, a Roman-surveying artefact. The nautical mile is about 15% longer, so a speed of one nautical mile per hour (one knot) is about 15% faster than one statute mile per hour. This catches people off guard: if a ferry captain announces "we're doing 30 knots," that's 34.5 mph — faster than it sounds to American ears.

How fast do common ships travel in mph?

A container ship at cruise: 20–25 knots = 23–29 mph. A cruise liner: 18–22 knots = 21–25 mph. A fast ferry (catamaran): 35–40 knots = 40–46 mph. A US Navy destroyer at flank speed: 30+ knots = 35+ mph (exact top speeds are classified). A recreational wake boat towing a rider: 18–22 knots = 21–25 mph. A 40-foot sportfisher at WOT (wide open throttle): 35–40 knots = 40–46 mph, burning roughly 200 litres per hour to stay there.

What's the mental shortcut for knots to mph?

Add 15%. Take the number of knots, move the decimal one place left (10%), halve that (5%), add both to the original. 20 knots: 2 + 1 = 3, so 23 mph. 50 knots: 5 + 2.5 = 7.5, so 57.5 mph. If you're on a boat and just need a rough sense: add a sixth. The error from the 15% shortcut is under 0.2 mph for anything under 30 knots. For exact work — navigation, passage planning, fuel calculations — use the converter above.

What went wrong with the Royal Majesty in 1995?

In June 1995, the cruise ship Royal Majesty ran aground on Rose and Crown Shoal off Nantucket Island, Massachusetts, with 1,509 people aboard. The NTSB investigation found that the GPS had been giving positions in nautical miles, but the watch officers assumed the readings were in statute miles. The ship was 17 miles off course — far enough to hit a shoal that charts showed 10 nautical miles away. The 15% difference between nautical and statute miles, multiplied across an entire voyage, accumulated into a navigation error large enough to ground a 568-foot vessel. No one was injured, but the ship sustained $7 million in damage. The incident is now a standard case study in maritime academies: always confirm which mile your instruments are using.

Engineering Context

The knot-to-mph conversion lives at an uncomfortable intersection of two communities that rarely talk to each other: mariners and drivers. Every NOAA weather radio broadcast gives wind speeds in knots. Every road sign in the United States gives speed limits in miles per hour. The 15% gap between them is small enough to ignore in casual conversation — "the wind's blowing about 20, so 23 mph, same thing" — and large enough to kill you if ignored in a navigation calculation. The most persistent source of confusion is recreational boating: GPS chartplotters sold in the US often default to mph for speed-over-ground because the manufacturer's user-research team concluded that American consumers "don't understand knots." But the charts those same plotters display are gridded in nautical miles. So the position is in one unit system and the speed is in another, and the boater is left to reconcile them in their head while bouncing around in a three-foot chop.

The fix is organisational, not mathematical: the US Coast Guard, the Royal Yachting Association, and every maritime training organisation on Earth recommend setting every instrument on the boat to knots and leaving it there. One unit system, end to end. The chart is in nautical miles, the speed is in knots, the wind is in knots, the VHF radio forecast is in knots — and the conversion to mph only happens once, at the dock, when someone asks "how fast does this boat go?"
