Why the Bar Refuses to Die

Every SI textbook calls the pascal the unit of pressure and the kilopascal its practical multiple. Every factory floor, dive boat, and tire shop still reads bar. The reason is ergonomics: sea-level atmosphere is 101.325 kPa — a number with three digits and a decimal — but it is almost exactly 1 bar. The bar was invented in 1909 precisely so meteorologists could write "1,000" instead of "101,325". When a machine operator sees 2.5 bar, they immediately know: two and a half atmospheres. When they see 250 kPa, they have to convert to know anything at all. This converter is the two-place decimal shift that keeps both languages honest.

The weather-map row carries the real story: "1013 hPa" is the same pressure as "1.013 bar" — the hectopascal is just the old millibar renamed in SI clothing, and the millibar was, in turn, one thousandth of Bjerknes's bar. Meteorology went metric without ever leaving the bar's scale. That is why a European forecast in hPa and an American barometer in inHg and an engineering drawing in kPa can all describe the same storm: each field picked the decimal position its audience reads most easily.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert kPa to bar?

Divide by 100: bar = kPa ÷ 100. A 250 kPa tire is 250 ÷ 100 = 2.5 bar. The factor is exact because the bar is defined as 100 kPa — the conversion is a two-place decimal shift.

What is the difference between hPa and kPa?

One hectopascal is 100 pascals, so 1 hPa = 0.1 kPa. Meteorology publishes pressure in hPa (the old 'millibar' renamed) — 1013 hPa on a weather map is 101.3 kPa or 1.013 bar. Engineering uses kPa, weather uses hPa, and the bar sits at the same 100 kPa scale: all three are the same pressure written in different decimal positions.

Why is bar still used if kPa is the SI unit?

Because the bar's size matches the atmosphere. One bar is almost exactly sea-level air pressure, so a mechanic, diver, or machine operator can read '2.5 bar' and know instantly it is about two and a half atmospheres. The kPa figure (250) is the same pressure but loses that intuitive anchor. Industry keeps both: SI for calculation, bar for communication.
