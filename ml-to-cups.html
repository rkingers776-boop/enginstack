mL to Cups: The Metric Baker Decoding an American Recipe, the 250 mL Cup That Isn't, and the Five Different Meanings of "One Cup" Across the World

You are standing in a kitchen in Berlin, or Melbourne, or Osaka. The recipe on your phone — an American chocolate chip cookie recipe with 14,000 five-star reviews — calls for "2¾ cups of all-purpose flour." You own a digital scale, a metric measuring jug, and a set of measuring spoons in mL. You do not own a cup measure. Your jug has a line that says "1 cup = 250 mL." The recipe's author measured her flour at roughly 130 grams per cup using the spoon-and-level method in a 236.6 mL cup. Your 250 mL cup will hold 5.7% more flour than hers. Every conversion from mL to cups requires you to first answer a question: which cup? The answer depends on which country the recipe was written in, which year it was published, and whether the author was using a scale or a scoop. The math is simple. The anthropology is not.

The Measuring Jug Deception: When "1 Cup" Printed on the Side Is Wrong

Walk into a kitchen supply store in London, Paris, or Sydney and buy a measuring jug. It will have mL markings on one side and "cup" markings on the other. The 250 mL line will be labeled "1 cup." This is the metric cup — exactly a quarter of a liter, adopted in the 1970s by Commonwealth countries that metricated and needed a round-number replacement for the Imperial cup (284 mL) they had just abandoned. The metric cup is clean, logical, and mathematically satisfying. It is also wrong for every American recipe on the internet.

An American recipe that calls for "1 cup of chicken stock" means 236.6 mL — not 250 mL. The 13.4 mL difference per cup sounds trivial. It isn't. In a risotto recipe calling for 6 cups of stock (1.42 L versus 1.50 L — the difference is 80 mL, or about a third of a cup), the extra liquid has to be boiled off. The rice cooks longer. The texture changes. In a cake recipe, an extra 5.7% of milk across 2 cups shifts the hydration ratio by enough to make the crumb noticeably different — not ruined, but not what the recipe intended. The European baker who fills to the 250 mL "1 cup" line on their jug for every "cup" in an American recipe is systematically adding 5.7% too much of every liquid and using a cup that holds 5.7% more volume for every dry ingredient. The errors compound. A recipe with 6 ingredients measured in cups is off by 5.7% on every one. The cake doesn't fail — it's just subtly, consistently wrong, and the baker never knows why.

Why Grams Beat mL for Anything That Isn't Water

The European baker who owns a digital scale — and most do, because European recipes are written in grams — doesn't need this converter for flour. They need it for liquids. A German recipe for pancake batter says "250 mL Milch." The American recipe says "1 cup milk." These are close — 250 vs. 236.6 mL — but the German batter is 5.7% wetter. The pancakes will be thinner. In a béchamel sauce with 2 cups of milk, the 27 mL difference changes the thickness from "coats the back of a spoon" to "pours like heavy cream." Cooking tolerates the error. Sauce-making notices it. Baking punishes it.

The permanent solution for dry ingredients: stop converting mL to cups entirely. Use grams. 1 US cup of all-purpose flour = 120–130 g (spooned and leveled) or 150–160 g (scooped). The recipe's author probably used 130 g. Pick a number and stay consistent across the entire recipe. The safest conversion for standard American all-purpose flour recipes: 125 g per cup, which splits the difference. Accept that the recipe's author almost certainly did not weigh their flour and the conversion is inherently approximate. If the recipe includes gram weights — and increasingly, American baking blogs do — use those instead. You are not the first metric baker to ask this question. The author probably added the weights because a thousand people before you asked.

Worked Examples

100 mL → 0.4227 cups (US)

100 ÷ 236.588 = 0.4227 cups. Slightly less than half a cup. Slightly more than 6 tablespoons (6 tbsp = 88.7 mL). The single espresso pulled as a doppio. A splash of cream in a sauce that "needs something."

250 mL → 1.057 cups (US)

The metric cup vs. the US cup, side by side. 250 mL is 1.057 US cups — meaning a 250 mL metric cup overflows a US cup by about 1 tablespoon. The most common conversion point of confusion in global recipe adaptation.

500 mL → 2.113 cups (US)

2 cups plus 2 tablespoons. The standard water bottle. Half a liter. In a bread recipe calling for 500 mL of water and 2 cups of flour, the 500 mL is actually 2.11 US cups — about 6% more water than if the flour cups were matched 1:1 with water cups. The hydration ratio matters.

1,000 mL → 4.227 cups (US)

1 liter. 4¼ cups, loosely. A Tetra Pak of stock. If an American recipe calls for 4 cups of stock and you pour in a full 1 L carton, you've added 53.6 mL too much — a bit over 3 tablespoons. For soup, irrelevant. For risotto, noticeable.

Engineering Context

The mL-to-cups conversion is a division by 236.5882365 — a factor derived from the US gallon (231 in³) divided by 16 cups per gallon. The precision of the conversion factor is not limited by measurement uncertainty: the inch is exactly 2.54 cm (1959 treaty), making the cubic inch exactly 16.387064 cm³, and therefore the cup exactly 236.5882365 mL. In practice, a kitchen scale with 1 g resolution cannot distinguish between 236.6 mL and 240 mL of water (a 3.4 g difference — a few drops). The 240 mL shortcut is operationally invisible at the home kitchen scale. The 250 mL metric cup error (13.4 mL, roughly 2.7 teaspoons) is visible but below the noise floor of most savory cooking. It only matters when multiplied across many cups or when the recipe's hydration ratio is already marginal. For the reverse direction, see cups to mL (1 US cup = 236.588 mL exactly). Frequently Asked Questions

My measuring jug says "1 cup = 250 mL." Is it wrong?

It is correct for the metric cup — the standard in Australia, New Zealand, and most metric countries. It is wrong for US recipes by 5.7%. The jug is not lying. It's using a different definition of "cup" than the American recipe you're trying to follow. The fix is not to throw out the jug. It's to ignore the "cup" markings entirely and use the mL scale. 1 US cup = 236.6 mL. Fill to that line on the mL scale. Your jug's cup markings are a convenience for metric-country recipes. They are a trap for American recipes. The jug manufacturer probably sold the same jug in both the US and international markets and only changed the printed scale.

Can I just treat 1 cup as 250 mL and adjust later?

For soups, stews, and most savory cooking: yes. The 5.7% error per cup is smaller than the variation in vegetable water content, evaporation rate, and "salt to taste." For baking, especially bread and pastry: no. A 5.7% hydration error in bread dough changes the crumb structure. For any recipe where the author included gram weights in addition to cups: the gram weight is the authoritative measurement. The cups were included for the American audience. The grams were included for you. When a recipe gives both, the grams always win.

Why don't American recipes just switch to milliliters?

Every American kitchen has measuring cups. Every American cookbook since Fannie Farmer (1896) uses cups. Every food blog, cooking magazine, and food package uses cups. Switching to mL means every recipe ever published in the United States becomes foreign to American home cooks. The benefit — compatibility with the metric world — accrues mostly to non-Americans. The cost — replacing the mental model of every American cook — falls entirely on Americans. American recipes increasingly include metric conversions in parentheses (driven by food blogs' international audiences), but the cup is not going anywhere. It's a 130-year-old standard with roughly 130 million daily users.

Should I convert mL to cups or just buy a set of US measuring cups?

If you cook American recipes regularly — more than once a month — buy a set of US measuring cups. They cost roughly $8 and last forever. They eliminate the arithmetic from every recipe. The cognitive load of dividing every "1½ cups of flour" by 236.588 in your head while not burning the garlic is not worth the $8 savings. If you cook American recipes occasionally — once a year for Thanksgiving — use this converter and your kitchen scale. The scale is the more versatile tool. It works for every cuisine, every recipe system. A set of US cups works for exactly one country's recipes. Buy whichever matches how often you encounter the problem.

What's the Japanese gō and why does it sometimes get called 200 mL when it's actually 180 mL?

The gō (合) is a traditional Japanese unit, legally defined since 1891 as approximately 180.39 mL — 1/10 of a shō. A Japanese rice cooker's included measuring cup holds 180 mL when filled to the brim. The "200 mL" figure sometimes quoted is the metric approximation used for convenience in international rice cooker manuals — a rounding that makes the arithmetic cleaner for non-Japanese markets. In practice: a "1 gō" portion of uncooked rice weighs roughly 150 g and yields roughly 330 g of cooked rice — one generous serving. Your rice cooker's "1 cup" line means 180 mL, not 200 and not 236.6. The word "cup" is doing a lot of work across a lot of cultures. It is not the same work in any two of them.
