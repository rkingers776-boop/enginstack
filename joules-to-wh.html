The Triangle Every Energy Engineer Carries

3.6 megajoules equals 1,000 watt hours equals 1 kilowatt hour. That one identity connects the three energy languages of engineering: the joule of physics, the watt hour of batteries, and the kilowatt hour of the electricity bill. Divide any joule figure by 3,600 and it lands in watt hours; divide again by 1,000 and it is kWh. The 3.6 MJ anchor is why this page exists — an energy analysis almost always produces megajoules, and the storage that must hold it is bought in watt hours or kilowatt hours. The conversion is exact because the watt hour is defined from the joule, not measured against it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert joules to watt hours?

Divide by 3,600: watt hours = joules ÷ 3,600. A 3.6 MJ physics result is 3,600,000 ÷ 3,600 = 1,000 Wh = 1 kWh. The factor is exact because 1 Wh is defined as 3,600 joules.

What is the 3.6 MJ anchor and why does it matter?

3.6 MJ is exactly 1 kWh — the electricity-bill unit. It connects the joule (physics), the watt hour (battery), and the kilowatt hour (utility) in one number: 3.6 MJ = 1,000 Wh = 1 kWh. Energy engineers remember this triangle the way mechanics remember 60 mph = 88 ft/s; it lets any energy figure be spoken in all three units at once.

Why would an engineer convert joules to watt hours?

Because the calculation is done in SI but the storage is bought in watt hours. A kinetic-energy or thermal analysis outputs joules or megajoules; the battery or flywheel that must hold that energy is rated in Wh. Converting the physics number to the storage rating is what turns a calculation into a purchase order.
