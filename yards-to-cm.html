Yards to Centimeters — the bolt of fabric that crosses an ocean

A US bolt of cloth is sold by the yard; the cutting room that receives it thinks in centimetres. Yards-to-cm is the single exact constant — 91.44 — that lets one bolt speak both languages, and it has been exact since 1959.

The Conversion That Ships American Fabric to the World

The American textile industry measures fabric in yards. The global garment industry — centered in Bangladesh, Vietnam, China, and Turkey — cuts and sews in centimeters. A pattern specification calling for 2.5 yards of 44-inch-wide cotton arrives at a Dhaka factory as 228.6 cm of 112 cm-wide cotton (44 inches = 111.76 cm, rounded to 112 cm for cutting tolerance). The conversion factor — 1 yard = 91.44 cm exactly — is embedded in every purchase order, every cutting ticket, and every quality-control spreadsheet that crosses the Pacific. A factory that accidentally uses 1 yard = 90 cm (a 1.6% error from rounding for convenience) will produce 50,000 shirts that are 2.5% smaller than spec. The retailer rejects the shipment. The factory eats the loss. The conversion error costs $200,000 — all because someone divided by 90 instead of 91.44.

Why 91.44, Not 90

cm = yards × 91.44

Derived directly from the 1959 yard definition: 1 yard = 0.9144 m = 91.44 cm. The number 91.44 is exact — it comes from 0.9144 × 100, with zero rounding. The temptation to round to 90 or even 91.5 is strong because 91.44 is awkward mental arithmetic. But in textile manufacturing, where fabric is cut to within 0.5 cm tolerance, rounding the conversion factor by 1.6% would produce a cumulative error of 40 cm over a 25-yard bolt — more than enough to scrap a pattern piece.

The Global Supply Chain That Runs on 91.44

A typical garment supply chain involves a designer in New York who specs fabric in yards, a fabric mill in North Carolina that produces bolts measured in yards, a cutting facility in Vietnam that lays up fabric in centimeters, and a quality-control auditor in Germany who measures finished garments in centimeters against a spec sheet that lists both units. Four countries, two measurement systems, one conversion factor. Every handoff in this chain is a potential error point. The designer writes "2 yd" on the spec sheet. The mill ships a bolt that says "2 yd (183 cm)" — already rounded, since 2 × 91.44 = 182.88 cm. The cutting facility measures 183 cm on the table. The QC auditor measures the finished garment at 181.5 cm after seam allowance and shrinkage. The 1.5 cm discrepancy launches an investigation that traces back to the mill's rounding of 182.88 to 183. The entire chain of custody for that missing 1.2 mm per yard is documented in a quality report that nobody reads until something goes wrong.

In retail, the conversion appears on every curtain package and every bedsheet label sold internationally. "84 inches (213 cm)" — that's 7 feet, or 2.33 yards. The metric conversion is provided because EU labeling laws require it, but the product was designed and manufactured in inches and yards. The centimeter number on the label is legally required, commercially irrelevant — but mathematically exact.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the garment industry still use yards when most of the world uses metric?

The US is the largest single market for apparel and the second-largest textile producer after China. American designers, pattern makers, and fabric mills all work in yards and inches. When a US brand contracts a factory in Vietnam to produce 100,000 units of a design, the tech pack (technical specification package) arrives in imperial units. The factory converts to metric. The finished goods are inspected in metric but labeled for the US market in imperial. The factory floor operates in metric; the brand's design studio operates in imperial. Both sides learn the conversion factors — 1 inch = 2.54 cm, 1 yard = 91.44 cm — because their paychecks depend on getting them right. The garment industry is functionally bilingual in measurement systems, and the yard-to-cm conversion is the first vocabulary lesson every production manager learns.

Is 1 yard closer to 90 cm or 100 cm?

Closer to 90 cm (91.44 cm actual). The difference between 1 yard and 1 meter is 8.56 cm, or about 3.4 inches. A yard is about a handspan shorter than a meter. This is why a "meter of fabric" (100 cm) is noticeably more material than a "yard of fabric" (91.44 cm) — about 9.4% more. In sewing, buying fabric by the meter instead of the yard gives you extra material; buying by the yard instead of the meter leaves you short. Always check which unit the pattern was designed for. A European pattern in meters converted to yards without the 1.094 factor (1 m = 1.094 yd) will produce a garment that's 9.4% too small.

How do I convert yards to cm for a sewing pattern that lists both imperial and metric?

If the pattern lists both units, trust the primary unit (the one the designer drafted in) and use the secondary unit as a check. Most commercial patterns from US brands (Simplicity, McCall's, Vogue) draft in imperial; the metric conversions are calculated and may include 0.5 cm rounding. For critical measurements (bodice length, sleeve length), convert yourself: yards × 91.44 = cm. For fabric yardage requirements, the pattern envelope's metric number is close enough — ±1 cm in total fabric length won't ruin the project. But for individual pattern pieces, convert precisely. A 0.5 cm difference in a shoulder seam across 8 pattern pieces can shift the fit by a full size.
