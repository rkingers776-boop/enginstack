43,560 — The Most Arbitrary Number in Real Estate

An acre is 43,560 square feet. Not 40,000. Not 50,000. 43,560. The number traces back to medieval England, where an acre was defined as the amount of land one yoke of oxen could plough in a single day — standardized as a strip one furlong (660 feet) long by one chain (66 feet) wide, yielding 43,560 sq ft. In 2026, that 13th-century agricultural unit still determines the size of every suburban lot in the United States, every farmland parcel, and every real estate tax assessment. A "quarter-acre block" (10,890 sq ft) is the default residential zoning unit in thousands of American towns. Nobody in the planning department knows why it's 43,560 — they just know the number.

The Division That Defines Suburbia

acres = sq ft ÷ 43,560

Simple division by an apparently arbitrary constant. But the constant is exact: 1 acre = 43,560 sq ft by US statute. Unlike meters-to-feet, which involves decimal places, this conversion is a whole-number ratio — one of the few in the imperial system. A 10,000 sq ft lot ÷ 43,560 = 0.2296 acres. A 43,560 sq ft lot = exactly 1 acre. Real estate agents round to two decimal places because nobody buys 0.2296 of an acre; they buy "about a quarter acre."

The Suburban Math That Shapes America

American suburban development follows the quarter-acre template: 10,890 sq ft per lot (43,560 ÷ 4). A 40-acre parcel (1,742,400 sq ft) subdivides into approximately 160 quarter-acre lots — minus land for roads, utilities, and setbacks, which typically consume 25-30% of the gross area. The developer starts with acres (the land purchase unit), converts to square feet (the planning and surveying unit), subdivides, and then markets the lots in acres (the buyer's unit) or square feet (the tax assessor's unit). Every step of the process runs through 43,560.

Zoning codes specify minimum lot sizes in square feet but define density in dwelling units per acre. A zone allowing 4 units per acre on a 100-acre tract permits 400 units — but only if the 43,560 sq ft per acre actually yields 400 buildable lots after subtracting roads, stormwater retention ponds, and common areas. The developer's feasibility study is fundamentally a sq-ft-to-acres conversion problem with a subtraction step in the middle. Getting it wrong by even 1% on a 100-acre project can mean three fewer buildable lots — roughly $150,000 in lost revenue at suburban land prices.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why isn't an acre a nice round number of square feet?

Because it wasn't designed by mathematicians — it was designed by oxen. The acre originates from the amount of land a team of oxen could plough in a day, which varied by soil type and terrain. The 43,560 number was standardized by English statute in the 13th century as 1 furlong (660 ft) × 1 chain (66 ft), both of which are nice round numbers in the surveyor's chain system (1 chain = 4 rods = 66 ft; 1 furlong = 10 chains = 660 ft). The chain was the standard surveyor's tool for centuries, and the acre was defined around it. The US adopted English land measurement wholesale, and by the time the metric system offered a more logical alternative (the hectare: 100 m × 100 m = 10,000 m²), 1.5 billion acres of American land had already been surveyed and titled using the old system. The conversion cost — re-surveying and re-titling an entire continent — was prohibitive. So 43,560 survives.

How do I figure out how many acres my property is from the square footage?

Divide the total square footage by 43,560. Most county assessor websites list both numbers. For a quick mental estimate: divide by 44,000 (error: -1%). Your 8,800 sq ft lot is 0.2 acres (8,800 ÷ 44,000 = 0.2; exact: 0.202 acres). The "divide by 44K" shortcut works because 43,560 is 98.9% of 44,000 — close enough for conversation. For legal documents (deeds, mortgages, tax assessments), always use the exact number from the survey or assessor's database. Never estimate an acreage for a legal purpose.

What's the difference between a US survey acre and an international acre?

They used to differ by about 2 parts per million because the US survey foot (1 ft = 1200/3937 m) was slightly different from the international foot (1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly). Since 1 acre = 43,560 sq ft, the difference in square meters was roughly 0.0008 m² per acre — invisible on a single lot but measurable over a county-sized parcel. NIST formally retired the US survey foot on January 1, 2023. All new surveys now use the international foot, and existing survey-acre deeds remain legally valid. The practical impact on a typical suburban homeowner: zero. On a 1,000-acre ranch: about 0.02 acres of re-measurement. For all practical purposes, the acre is now unified globally under the international foot definition.
