How to Convert kW to HP

Converting kilowatts to horsepower is the most common power unit translation in the global automotive and industrial world — it's how you read a European EV spec sheet (150 kW = 201 HP), how an American engineer sizes an IEC motor (7.5 kW = 10.1 HP), and why a German BMW 330i rated at 190 kW is marketed as 255 HP in the US. 1 kW = 1.34102 mechanical HP. Multiply kW by 1.341. The kW is the SI unit of power and is used universally in electrical engineering, while HP persists in automotive marketing and US industrial equipment. This duality means every international procurement document, every cross-border machine specification, and every EV review bridges these two units.

The Conversion Formula

HP = kW × 1.34102

Example 1: Convert 100 kW to HP

100 × 1.341 = 134.1 HP (a typical 2.0L turbocharged petrol engine)

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert kW to HP?

Multiply kilowatts by 1.34102. Formula: HP = kW × 1.34102. For a quick estimate, multiply by 4/3 (kW × 1.333), which gives a ~0.6% underestimate — close enough for casual comparison.

Why are EVs rated in kW but gasoline cars in HP?

Electric motors are fundamentally electrical devices, so their power is naturally measured in kW. Gasoline engines were historically rated in HP because James Watt invented the unit to sell steam engines against horses. As EVs become dominant, some automakers (Tesla, Porsche) now show kW in the UI and HP only in marketing — a tacit admission that kW is the more honest unit.

Is the kW rating of an EV motor the same as its HP rating?

Yes, it's the same physical quantity. 150 kW = 201 HP. However, EV power ratings are often "continuous" or "30-minute" ratings, while gasoline engines quote "peak" HP at a specific RPM. An EV's 150 kW motor can deliver 150 kW from 0 to ~8,000 RPM, while a 201 HP gasoline engine only hits 201 HP at ~6,000 RPM — below that it produces less.

Engineering Context

The kW-to-HP conversion is embedded in every international supply chain. An ABB IE5 ultra-premium efficiency motor rated 11 kW (14.7 HP) draws 11.3 kW at full load (97.3% efficiency), while a comparable NEMA Premium 15 HP motor draws 12.1 kW at full load (92.5% efficiency). Over 8,000 operating hours per year at $0.10/kWh, the 11 kW motor saves 6,400 kWh — 640/year — despite being "smaller" (14.7 HP vs 15 HP). The kW rating tells you the truth; the HP rounding hides it. In power generation, a Caterpillar 3516C diesel genset is rated 2,000 kW (2,682 HP) standby — but kW governs because the alternator produces kW directly, and HP is derivative. The genset's fuel consumption is 518 L/hr at full load, so each kWh costs ~$0.39 in diesel alone (at $1.50/L) — the HP number (0.193 L/HP-hr) is used only for comparing to legacy engines. In data center design, the kW is absolute: a 10 MW data center is 13,410 HP, but nobody says "13,410 HP data center" because the power doesn't turn a shaft — it powers servers. The distinction between kW (real power) and kVA (apparent power) adds another layer: a UPS rated 100 kVA at 0.9 power factor delivers 90 kW = 120.7 HP of real power, but the 100 kVA number is what sizes the breaker. Every hyperscale data center PUE calculation starts with IT load in kW — and the HP conversion is a historical footnote, used only when the backup generators are diesel (a 3 MW diesel genset = 4,023 HP). The EU directive 2005/32/EC mandated kW as the primary power rating for all electric motors sold in Europe — the HP equivalent is now only shown in parentheses, if at all. But in the US, NEMA MG 1 still uses HP as the primary rating. The single number 1.34102 thus sits at the center of a $100B/year global motor market, quietly translating every catalogue entry across the Atlantic.
