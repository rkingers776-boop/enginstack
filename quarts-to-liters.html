Quarts to Liters: The 0.946 Factor That Makes a Quart "Almost" a Liter — and Why "Almost" Overfills Your Engine

At the auto parts store, a bottle of 5W-30 says 1 QT / 946 mL on the label. The number 946 is not arbitrary. It is 1/4 of 3,785 — the milliliters in a US gallon. A quart is 0.946352946 liters exactly. Close enough to a liter that most people treat them as the same unit. Close enough that a 5-quart engine sump takes 4.73 liters, not 5. Pour in a full 5-liter jug and you have overfilled by 0.27 liters — more than a cup of extra oil, enough to froth the crankshaft at high RPM. The quart and the liter have been neighbors on the same shelf for 50 years. They still disagree by 5.4%.

Where the Quart Comes From: One Quarter of a Wine Cylinder

The quart is exactly what its name suggests: a quarter of a gallon. But which gallon? The US liquid quart derives from the US liquid gallon — the 1707 Queen Anne wine gallon of 231 cubic inches. One quart = 57.75 cubic inches. After the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement defined 1 inch = 2.54 cm exactly, a cubic inch became exactly 16.387064 cm³. So 57.75 × 16.387064 = 946.352946 cm³, and since 1 cm³ = 1 mL by the 1964 CGPM resolution, a quart is 0.946352946 liters. Not 0.95. Not 0.946. The full decimal chain matters when you multiply it by 55 gallons in a drum.

The quart is the most common volume unit in American kitchens after the cup. A quart of milk. A quart of chicken stock. A quart of takeout soup — the familiar cylindrical cardboard container with the wire handle, which holds exactly 32 fluid ounces (one quart) and has been manufactured to the same dimensions since the 1920s. That container is a quart. In metric, it is 946 mL. The container mold hasn't changed. The ink on the bottom just reads both numbers now.

There is also a US dry quart — 1.101220942715 liters — used for measuring produce, grain, and dry goods. It is exactly 1/32 of a US bushel. The dry quart appears on roadside farm stand signs ("Peaches — $3/quart") and in USDA crop reports. It is not the quart this page converts. This page converts the liquid quart — the one in your refrigerator, your engine, and your measuring cup. The dry quart is a different unit that happens to share a name. Using the wrong one to convert a peach basket to liters adds a 16% error — large enough that a pallet of blueberries mislabeled by a factor of 1.101 would fail a customs inspection. For the dry quart conversion, see the Volume & Cooking Guide.

The Engine Oil Trap: When "Five Quarts" and "Five Liters" Collide

A 2018 Toyota Camry takes 4.8 quarts of oil — which is 4.5 liters. A 2020 Honda Civic takes 3.7 quarts — 3.5 liters. A Ford F-150 with the 5.0L V8 takes 8.8 quarts — 8.3 liters. The quart and the liter are close but not interchangeable, and the difference compounds: every 4 quarts you pour, thinking they're liters, adds roughly 215 mL of extra oil. In an 8-quart engine, that's nearly half a liter overfilled. Overfilled oil contacts the spinning crankshaft, whips into a froth, and the oil pump — designed to pump liquid, not foam — sends aerated oil to the bearings. Aerated oil does not lubricate. It compresses. Bearings starve. The engine seizes. The failure is blamed on "oil pump failure" or "bearing defect" because nobody checks the oil level after filling — they poured "exactly what the manual said." The manual said quarts. They bought liters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is an American quart smaller than a liter?

The quart is 1/4 of a gallon, and the US gallon (3.785 L) is smaller than 4 liters. The liter was defined independently — originally as the volume of 1 kg of water at 4°C (1901), later unified with the cubic decimeter (1964). There was no intent to align the quart to the liter. They evolved from different measurement traditions: the quart from the Queen Anne wine gallon of 1707, the liter from the French Republican decimal reforms of 1795. The coincidence that they're within 5.4% is just that — a coincidence. It has persisted because neither the US nor the metric world saw enough benefit in harmonizing a 5% gap to justify retooling every liquid container on the continent.

When do I need exact quart-to-liter conversion vs. rounding?

For cooking: rounding 1 qt ≈0.95 L is fine for soups, stews, and braises — the 5 mL difference per quart is less than the evaporation loss during simmering. For baking liquids (milk in a cake batter), use the exact 0.94635 factor — milk quantity affects hydration and crumb structure. For engine oil: use the exact factor, always. For chemical solutions in a lab: use the exact factor, because 5.4% concentration error in a reagent can shift a titration endpoint. For paint: the hardware store's tinting machine measures in fractions of a fluid ounce. The quart-to-liter step there affects the color formula — but paint color is corrected by eye at the mixing counter, so the conversion is absorbed by the process. The rule: if the result goes into an engine or a chemical reaction, use exact. If it goes into soup, round.

Is a quart of water the same as a liter of water in weight?

No. A quart of water at 4°C weighs about 0.946 kg. A liter of water at 4°C weighs exactly 1 kg (by the original 1795 definition — though modern metrology has de-coupled the liter from the kilogram, the difference is less than 30 parts per million). So a quart of water is 5.4% lighter than a liter. In cooking, this matters only for recipes that list liquid ingredients by weight — which is standard in professional kitchens and European recipes, but rare in American home cooking, where liquids are measured by volume. Engineering Context

The US liquid quart is defined as exactly 1/4 US gallon = 57.75 cubic inches = 946.352946 cm³ = 0.946352946 L. The chain is: 1 gallon (231 in³) →1 quart (1/4 gal, 57.75 in³) →1 pint (1/2 qt, 28.875 in³) →1 cup (1/2 pt, 14.4375 in³) →1 fl oz (1/8 cup, 1.8046875 in³). Every unit in this chain is exact — no measured factors, no API gravity corrections, no temperature dependence. For custody transfer of petroleum products, however, volume is corrected to 60°F (API Manual of Petroleum Measurement Standards), which introduces thermal expansion factors that can shift results by several percent. This page converts the volume units, not the temperature-corrected equivalents. For industrial fluid metering, always apply the relevant API or ISO temperature correction after the unit conversion, not before.
